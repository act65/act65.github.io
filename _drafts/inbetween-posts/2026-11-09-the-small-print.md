In 2005 a software company called PC Pitstop put a clause near the end of its licence agreement offering money to anyone who wrote in and asked for it. It took five months and more than three thousand sales before anyone did. Doug Heckman, who did, was sent a thousand dollars. In 2019 the travel insurer Squaremouth tried the same thing: Donelan Andrews, a teacher in Georgia, read her policy, found the offer, and was sent ten thousand.

That is the cheerful version. Most of the history of the small print is less cheerful. It is a long record of one party writing the terms and the other party agreeing to them, and of what happens in the gap between what was written and what was understood. Some of the gaps are jokes, some are mistakes, and some are what an empire looks like at the level of a sentence.

I've grouped the examples by mechanism rather than by date, because the mechanisms repeat.

## Nobody reads them

The jokes first, because they show the baseline.

- **Souls.** On 1 April 2010 the British games retailer GameStation added a clause to its online terms granting it "a non transferable option to claim, for now and for ever more, your immortal soul." Customers could opt out by ticking a box. About one in eight did. GameStation said it was a joke and would not be enforcing it.
- **First-borns.** In 2014 the security firm F-Secure set up free Wi-Fi in London whose terms asked users to give up their eldest child "for the duration of eternity." Six people agreed before the experiment was stopped. In 2016 the researchers Jonathan Obar and Anne Oeldorf-Hirsch built a fake social network called NameDrop, with a first-born clause buried in its terms of service. Nearly all of their few hundred participants signed up without noticing it. They titled the paper "The Biggest Lie on the Internet."
- **Toilets.** In 2017 Purple, a Wi-Fi provider, added a "community service clause" to its terms as a two-week experiment. Twenty-two thousand people agreed to up to a thousand hours of work, including cleaning portable toilets at festivals and "manually relieving sewer blockages." One person found it.
- **Zombies and missiles.** Not every strange clause is a joke. In 2016 Amazon's terms for Lumberyard, its game engine, said it couldn't be used in life-critical systems such as medical equipment or air traffic control, except in the event of a widespread viral outbreak that causes corpses to "reanimate and seek to consume living human flesh." Apple's iTunes terms forbid using the software to develop nuclear, missile, chemical or biological weapons. That one sounds like the joke and isn't: it's ordinary export-control language, pasted in because the law requires it to be somewhere.

None of these clauses would have been enforced, and in most places none of them could have been. But they measure something real: the terms are not read, and the people who write them know it. Every other example below happens on top of that fact.

## The lion's share

The problem is old enough that Roman law had a name for it. In Aesop's fable the lion, the fox and the donkey go hunting together, and the lion takes every portion of the kill. A *societas leonina*, a lion's partnership, was a partnership in which one partner took all the profit and the other bore all the loss. The Roman jurists held that it wasn't a partnership at all and refused to enforce it.

Law has been naming particular clauses as void ever since, usually decades after they did their damage. The pattern repeats in the examples below: the clause is invented, it spreads, people are ruined by it, and eventually it gets a name and a statute.

## Terms on the back of the ticket

The modern standard-form contract grew up with the railways. A ticket, a cloakroom stub or a bill of lading came with conditions printed on the back, and English and American courts spent the next century arguing about when a person who never turned the ticket over was bound by them.

- In *Parker v South Eastern Railway* (1877) a man left a bag in a station cloakroom. His ticket said on the back that the railway's liability was limited to ten pounds, and the bag was lost. The Court of Appeal said the question was whether the railway had done enough to bring the conditions to his notice, not whether he had read them.
- In *Spurling v Bradshaw* (1956) Lord Denning added a sliding scale: the more unusual or onerous the clause, the more it has to be pointed out. "Some clauses which I have seen would need to be printed in red ink on the face of the document with a red hand pointing to it before the notice could be held to be sufficient." Lawyers still call this the red hand rule.
- In *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking* (1971) the ticket came out of a machine at the entrance to a car park, and the conditions were on a sign inside. Denning held that the contract was made when the machine took the money, so terms the driver could only see afterwards weren't part of it.

In the United States, Edwin Patterson borrowed the French term *contrat d'adhésion* in a 1919 Harvard Law Review article on life-insurance policies: a contract one side drafts and the other can only adhere to. It's still the standard name. The red hand rule is close to the question this whole essay keeps coming back to. A clause binds you if someone took reasonable steps to tell you about it, and what counts as reasonable has been shrinking ever since: from a red hand, to a sign, to a link, to a box you tick without reading.

## The clause that takes everything back

**Acceleration and the snatch-back.** In 1930s Britain, furniture and household goods were sold on hire purchase. Technically you were renting them until the last payment, when they became yours. Miss one payment, and the hire-purchase company could take the goods back, keep everything you'd paid, and resell them. It was called the "snatch-back," and some companies seem to have targeted buyers who were near the end of their payments. The Hire-Purchase Act of 1938, a private member's bill from the Labour MP Ellen Wilkinson, made it illegal to repossess without a court order once a third of the price had been paid.

The same move turns up wherever there's instalment credit. One late payment makes the whole remainder due at once, plus fees.

**The truck system and the company store.** If your wages are paid in tokens that are only good at your employer's shop, your employer sets your prices too. Britain's Truck Act of 1831 required wages to be paid in coin. American coal towns ran company stores well into the twentieth century. In sharecropping in the post-war American South, the landowner kept the books, the cropper often couldn't read them, and at the yearly "settle" the cropper was told what he owed.

## The contract you didn't understand

**Indenture.** Between 1879 and 1916 around sixty thousand Indians were taken to Fiji to work on sugar plantations under five-year indentures. The labourers called the contract the *girmit*, from the English word "agreement," and called themselves *girmitiya*: people of the agreement. The recruiters (*arkatis*) were paid per head and routinely misdescribed the work, the wages and the place. A contract existed, and it had been signed. The terms that mattered were the ones nobody had said out loud, like the penal clauses that made leaving work a crime.

In the same decades, Chinese labourers were shipped to Cuba under eight-year contracts written in Spanish. In 1874 a Chinese government commission collected over a thousand depositions from them in Cuba. They describe contracts signed under deception or force, wages docked for clothing and food, and contracts extended once the eight years were up.

## Two texts

The cleanest version of the gap is when there are literally two contracts.

**The Treaty of Waitangi (1840).** The British drafted the treaty in English, and the missionary Henry Williams and his son translated it into Māori overnight. The two texts don't say the same thing.

- In the English text, the chiefs cede "all the rights and powers of Sovereignty." The Māori text uses *kawanatanga*, a missionary-coined word built on "governor," closer to "governance" than to sovereignty.
- The English text guarantees "full exclusive and undisturbed possession of their Lands and Estates Forests Fisheries and other properties." The Māori text guarantees *tino rangatiratanga*, full chieftainship, over their lands, villages and *taonga*, all their treasured things. That is a much wider promise. In the twentieth century it became the basis for claims over the Māori language and, later, over radio spectrum.

Around five hundred chiefs signed, and almost all of them signed the Māori text. The Crown then governed according to the English one. In 2014 the Waitangi Tribunal concluded that the chiefs who signed in the north had not ceded sovereignty. (I wrote a story that re-runs this, with humanity on the other side of the table: [Wai-terra](/alien-treaty/).)

**The Treaty of Wuchale (1889).** Italy and Ethiopia signed a treaty in Italian and Amharic. In Article 17, the Amharic text said the Emperor Menelik II *could* use Italy's government for his dealings with other powers if he wished. The Italian text said he *must*, which made Ethiopia an Italian protectorate. Italy notified the other European powers on the strength of its version. Menelik found out, protested, and renounced the treaty. The disagreement ended in war, and in 1896 Ethiopia defeated Italy at Adwa. That's one of the rare cases where the side that couldn't read the other text won.

**The Requerimiento (1513).** This is the extreme of the two-text problem: one text and no listener. Spanish conquistadors were required to read a declaration to the people they met, telling them to accept the authority of the Church and the Spanish crown or face war, enslavement and the loss of their property, "and the deaths and losses which shall accrue from this are your fault." It was read in Spanish, sometimes from ships, sometimes to villages that had already been emptied. Its real audience was the Spanish legal system, which needed the notice to have been given.

The modern version of this is mild by comparison, but it is everywhere. Contracts in two languages often say which one governs, and the "translation provided for convenience only" is the other one.

## The rules that only bind one side

**Unequal treaties.** After the First Opium War, the Treaty of Nanking (1842) and the supplementary Treaty of the Bogue (1843) gave Britain two clauses worth knowing.

- **Most-favoured-nation treatment.** Any privilege China later granted to another power would automatically extend to Britain too. Other powers then got the same clause, so every concession to one became a concession to all.
- **Extraterritoriality.** British subjects in China were tried by British consuls under British law, not by Chinese courts. The law of the land did not apply to them.

Japan signed similar treaties with the Western powers in 1858, and spent the next four decades rebuilding its legal system on European lines specifically to get rid of them. Extraterritoriality in Japan ended in 1899. In China it lasted until 1943.

## Omissions, commas, and time bombs

Sometimes the trouble is what the contract leaves out, or the punctuation.

- **The comma.** In 2006 a dispute between Rogers Communications and Aliant over a contract for utility poles in Canada came down to a single comma. The first ruling read it as letting Aliant end the contract early, which would have cost Rogers about two million Canadian dollars. Rogers then pointed out that the French version of the contract, which was equally binding, had no such ambiguity, and the ruling was reversed. Two texts again, but for once the second text saved someone.
- **The Oxford comma.** Maine's overtime law listed the exempt activities as "canning, processing, preserving, freezing, drying, marketing, storing, packing for shipment or distribution of" perishable foods. Delivery drivers for Oakhurst Dairy argued that, without a comma after "shipment," the exemption covered *packing* for distribution, not distribution itself, so the drivers were owed overtime. The court agreed in 2017, and Oakhurst settled for five million dollars.
- **The ninety-nine-year lease.** In 1898 Britain leased the New Territories next to Hong Kong from China for ninety-nine years. Hong Kong Island itself had been ceded permanently, but by the 1980s the colony couldn't function without the leased land. The date written into the 1898 lease set the terms of the 1997 handover.

## When the buyer reads the terms

The other direction is rarer, and it tends to make the law.

- **Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Company (1893).** The company advertised that it would pay £100 to anyone who caught influenza after using its smoke ball as directed, and said it had deposited money in a bank to show it was serious. Louisa Carlill used the ball, caught flu and sued. The court held that the advertisement was a real offer and she had accepted it by using the product. Law students still learn contract formation from this case.
- **Hoover's free flights (1992).** Hoover's UK arm offered two free return flights to Europe, and later the United States, with any purchase of over £100. People bought vacuum cleaners they didn't want to get flights worth far more. The cost ran to tens of millions of pounds (estimates vary). It went on for years in the courts and cost Hoover's European business dearly.
- **The Pepsi Harrier jet (1996).** A Pepsi advert jokingly priced a Harrier jump jet at seven million Pepsi Points. Pepsi's own rules let you buy missing points for ten cents each. John Leonard raised the money and sent a cheque. The court held in 1999 that the advert was not an offer, and that no reasonable person would think it was.
- **Shylock.** The literary version of this, from *The Merchant of Venice*: the bond grants a pound of flesh, Portia reads it more carefully than Shylock did, and points out that it mentions no blood. The person who reads the terms best wins. Whether that's justice depends on who had the better reader.

## Now

The modern examples don't need a joke, because they work in plain sight.

- In 2009 Amazon remotely deleted copies of Orwell's *1984* from customers' Kindles, after finding the publisher didn't hold the rights.
- In 2020 Tesla removed paid Autopilot features from a used car after it was resold, on the grounds that the features belonged to the original buyer.
- Subprime car loans in the US often come with remote "starter interrupt" devices, so a missed payment can mean a car that won't start.
- In 2024 Disney argued that a widower's wrongful-death claim over a restaurant at one of its parks belonged in arbitration, because he had once signed up for a Disney+ free trial. Disney backed down after the coverage.

The common thread is a licence presented as a purchase, plus terms that reach further than anyone would have guessed.

## What I take from it

I'm not sure there's a lesson here so much as a shape. The party who drafts the terms, and the party who reads them best, tends to win. Sometimes that's the same party. When it's the weaker one, as with Carlill, Leonard and Doug Heckman, it becomes a story because it's unusual. Law tends to catch up with particular clauses, but slowly, and one at a time: the lion's share, truck wages, the snatch-back, the comma.

One thing seems to have changed. For most of this history, reading carefully was slow and expensive, so only the powerful side could afford it. If reading becomes cheap, if everyone has a bot that reads every page, it isn't obvious the gap closes. It may just move to whoever has the better bot. That's the premise of a story I'm working on.

The boom gate held onto his ticket and thought about it for a while before it lifted, which was the same as last time and the time before that, and Dean sat with his hands on the wheel and said something about it under his breath.

Third bay along, under the tree, because the tree meant the ute wouldn't be an oven at two o'clock. Eight weeks he'd been parking under that tree. Nobody else seemed to have worked it out.

Day Stay was up one floor. He took the stairs.

***

Chair six, by the window, which he'd asked for the first time and had ever since without asking. He put his phone face-down on the arm and got his boots up on the footrest and settled in for an hour and a half of nothing.

Thursday was the thing on his mind. The trade academy did a prizegiving at the end of every intake and they'd asked him to talk for ten minutes, and he'd said yes in March when Thursday was a long way off, and now it was Thursday in two days and he had about four sentences.

What he wanted to say to them was this: nobody is coming.

That was it, really. That was the whole of it. Not the council, not the polytech, not your old man, not the bloke who says he'll put work your way. Nobody. And that isn't the bad news, that's the good news, because the day you stop waiting is the day you start. He'd worked that out at seventeen, standing outside a merchant's yard with a list he couldn't pay for, and it had not once let him down since. Eleven people on the books now. Two vans. A yard of his own with his name on the gate, and every single thing behind that gate he had put there himself.

He'd say it better than that on Thursday. But that was the shape of it.

***

"Morning, Dean." Moana had the trolley. "How've you been?"

"Yeah, good."

"Any bugs? Anything since last time — sore throat, chest, anything you've been to the doctor about?"

"Nope."

She was already going. Blood pressure cuff on the right arm, and the clip on his left index finger, and she looked at the screen and not at him while it settled.

The clip is a plastic peg with a light in it. Takuo Aoyagi was trying to cancel a wobble in a cardiac output measurement, an artefact caused by the pulse, which was in his way. He worked out that the thing in his way was carrying the information. He was not much recognised for it in his lifetime and he died in April of 2020, which was the month the whole world went looking for the thing he had made.

"Ninety-eight," Moana said. "Lovely. Arm up."

***

The speech needed a story in it. They liked a story. He had the one about the first winter, the van with the door that didn't shut, the tarpaulin. Kids liked that one. He'd been twenty-two and he'd done a full bathroom in Naenae with a borrowed set of pipe grips and no idea what he was doing, and he'd made every mistake there was to make on that job and had paid for all of them out of his own pocket, and that was the education. Nobody hands you that. You buy it.

Then Grace came over with the bag and the two of them did the thing they always did.

"Dean Whitcombe."

"Dean Whitcombe."

"Date of birth."

He gave it.

"Infliximab, four hundred milligrams, in two hundred and fifty of sodium chloride." Grace held the bag up so Moana could see the label, and Moana read it back off the label and not off the chart, and then they both signed.

Every eight weeks, twice, out loud, like a call and response. He had asked once, years ago, why two of them. Because it's the rule, someone had said.

Wayne Jowett was eighteen. He was in remission. In January of 2001, at the Queen's Medical Centre in Nottingham, a drug that was supposed to go into a vein went into his spine instead, because the two syringes fitted the same port and looked much the same and there was nobody standing there saying the words out loud.

"All good," said Grace. "You're in."

***

The line went in first go, which it didn't always. Moana wiped the back of his hand with a little foil sachet before she went near it and the cold of it stayed there a second after she'd finished.

Ignaz Semmelweis made the students on his ward wash their hands in chlorinated lime, because they came to the women straight from the dissecting room, and the women in their ward were dying at several times the rate of the women in the ward run by the midwives. The dying stopped, more or less, immediately. He could not explain why and would not pretend to, and he was difficult about it for years, and then not difficult but abusive, writing open letters to the obstetricians of Europe calling them murderers. Every one of them a man who could have helped him. He was put in an asylum in 1865, and beaten by the guards, and died two weeks later of an infection in his hand.

Dean watched the first of it go down the line and thought about whether to mention money on Thursday. Probably not. They were seventeen. They'd hear it as bragging.

***

Forty minutes to go on the pump.

He read the label because it was in front of him and there was nothing else. There was his name on it, printed, and a lot number, and an expiry.

In 1937 a company in Tennessee put sulfanilamide into a syrup so children would take it, and the syrup was diethylene glycol, and it killed over a hundred people and most of them were children. The company had tested it for flavour. Nobody had asked them to test it for anything else. Harold Watkins mixed it. He shot himself before it went to court.

The lot number was eight characters. Dean looked at it for a while.

And then, without particularly deciding to, he started doing a takeoff.

It was the only way he knew to look at a thing. You stand in a room and you list what it takes: the pipe, the fittings, the labour, the hire, the consent, the make-good. He was good at it. He was, and he'd say this to anyone, the best in the district at it.

So: the bag. Somebody made the bag. Somebody made the plastic and somebody worked out that it had to be that plastic and not another one. The drug in it — an antibody, grown, not mixed. Somebody had worked out how to make a cell line that would go on making one antibody forever, and he looked that up on his phone with one thumb because his left hand had a line in it.

Two men in a lab in Cambridge in 1975. One of them, Milstein, had left Argentina eleven years earlier when the institute he worked at was broken up by the government. They were not trying to make a medicine. They were trying to understand how the body manages to produce so many different antibodies, which was an argument going on at the time, and the method was a thing they built to settle it. The British research body decided the method was not worth patenting. Nobody was paid a cent for it and other people made a great deal.

Right. So: the two of them. And whoever paid the electricity in that building. And whoever kept the mice, because there were mice, there had to have been mice, some bloke going in on a Sunday to keep the mice alive. And whoever taught the two of them, and whoever taught them.

And the pump. Somebody worked out that a drip should be a machine that counts rather than a bloke watching a clock. And the cannula. And the tape.

And the tree in the car park, which the council planted and somebody waters.

He got about that far and stopped, and he stopped because it was not closing. He had done a thousand takeoffs and every one of them closed. You get to the bottom of the list and you total it and you put your name on it. This one went sideways at every line and each line went sideways again and there was no bottom to it anywhere. It was, he thought, an unfinishable job, and he sat with a mild professional irritation for about a minute before something under the irritation moved.

He had already had all of it. That was the thing. He had it before he'd decided anything. He'd had the sugar on the plastic spoon in about 1974, and he did not remember it, and there was a boy in front of him in the line crying about it, and that was all he had, that one second of another kid crying, and he'd been given the rest of it without ever once being asked.

***

Twelve minutes.

He thought about Thursday and found he had lost the run of the four sentences. He got them back but they'd gone stiff on him, the way a line goes stiff when you've been reading it too long, and he could not tell any more whether it was true.

It was true. That was the thing. Nobody had come. Not one person had come. He had stood outside that merchant's yard with a list and a hundred and forty dollars and no one had come, and everything through the gate with his name on it he had put there.

Both of those, at once, and he could not make them sit.

***

The pump did its beeping and Moana came and took the line out and put a cotton ball on the back of his hand and told him to press.

"Cheers," Dean said. And then, because it wasn't enough and he could hear that it wasn't: "No, thanks. Thank you. For — " and he moved the cotton ball slightly, meaning the chair, the bag, the two of them saying his name out loud, the peg on his finger, all of it.

Moana looked up.

"I just do the obs," she said, and smiled, and took the trolley to chair seven.

***

He stood in the corridor doing his sleeve up.

There's a door halfway along, a heavy one, no window, and it was open, held there by a grey plastic wedge somebody had kicked under it. It's open every time. He'd walked past it every eight weeks for six years.

FIRE DOOR — KEEP SHUT.

The Victoria Hall in Sunderland had a door at the bottom of the stairs that had been bolted so that it would open only a few inches, so that the children could be let through one at a time and their tickets checked. Then there were a lot of children on the stairs at once. It was June of 1883 and they had been promised a toy on the way out.

Dean stood there a moment. Then he put his boot against the wedge and shunted it out from under, and the door swung to on its closer and clicked, and the corridor was a bit darker, and nobody came out to see who had done it and nobody was going to.

He read the sign again, the whole thing, the five words, which he had never in his life read as words.

Then he went down and paid four dollars for the tree.

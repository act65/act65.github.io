In 2002 Anssi Vanjoki, then a director at Nokia, was caught riding his motorbike at 75 km/h in a 50 zone in Helsinki. He was fined 116,000 euros. Finland sets many fines as a number of "day fines," each worth a fraction of the offender's daily income, and Vanjoki's income was large. Most countries would have fined him the same flat amount as anyone else, which for him would have been a rounding error.

Which of those is equal treatment? The flat fine treats everyone the same on paper. The Finnish one tries to make the fine hurt everyone about the same. I think the gap between those two kinds of equality runs through most of the law, and it's wider than it looks.

## What the rule of law promises

One of A. V. Dicey's three meanings of the rule of law, in 1885, was equality before the law: every person, whatever their rank, is subject to the same law and the same ordinary courts. It's one of the assumptions a democracy rests on. If laws are made by everyone's representatives, they should at least apply to everyone.

The standard reply came six years later from Anatole France: "The law, in its majestic equality, forbids rich and poor alike to sleep under bridges, to beg in the streets, and to steal bread." The law can be perfectly equal in what it says and still land very differently, depending on who you are.

In 1910 the American jurist Roscoe Pound called this the difference between "law in books" and "law in action." This post is about one particular source of that difference: **most legal protections don't apply themselves.** Someone has to invoke them, and invoking them costs time, knowledge and money.

## A fine is a price

In a well-known study (Gneezy and Rustichini, 2000), ten day-care centres in Haifa had a problem with parents picking their children up late. Six of them introduced a small fine for lateness. Late pickups went *up*, roughly doubling. When the fines were removed, lateness stayed high. The paper's title is the explanation: "A Fine Is a Price." Before the fine, lateness was a small breach of trust with the teachers. After it, lateness was a service you could buy.

For someone rich enough, a lot of law works like this. A parking fine is a parking fee. A penalty for missing a filing deadline is a cost of doing business. This isn't evasion exactly; the law is applied, and the fine is paid. But the law was written as a prohibition, and it reaches some people as a price list.

Day fines are one attempt to fix it. Germany uses them for criminal fines (*Tagessätze*). Its traffic fines, like most countries', are flat.

## Rights you have to invoke

Fines are the visible case. The less visible one, and I think the more important, is the long list of rights that exist only if you claim them.

Compare two kinds of protection:

- **Automatic protections apply whether or not you do anything.** German law simply voids certain unfair clauses in standard contracts (§ 307 BGB). Brazil's consumer code does the same (article 51). A minimum wage written into payroll software is paid without anyone asking.
- **Invoked protections exist only once you act on them.** Getting a deposit back, claiming under a warranty, withdrawing from a contract, contesting a dismissal, disputing a bill, appealing a benefits decision.

To use an invoked protection you have to:
1. know the right exists,
2. notice that it applies to you,
3. act before the deadline (in the UK, a claim for unfair dismissal usually has to start within three months),
4. do it in the right form,
5. afford the cost of the dispute, and
6. be able to take the risk of losing, or of the other side retaliating.

Each step filters people out, and the filters are correlated with money. Lawyers help at every step, and lawyers cost money. So does the free time to do it yourself.

The scale of this is measurable. In 2022 the US Legal Services Corporation surveyed low-income Americans and found they got no legal help, or not enough, for 92% of their substantial civil legal problems: evictions, debt, benefits, custody. The rights existed in most of those cases. They just weren't used.

## Repeat players

Marc Galanter's 1974 paper "Why the 'Haves' Come Out Ahead" explains why the gap compounds over time. He split litigants into **one-shotters**, who meet the legal system rarely (a tenant, a divorcing couple, a car-crash victim), and **repeat players**, who meet it constantly (landlords, insurers, banks, the state).

Repeat players have obvious advantages: specialist lawyers, experience, and the ability to spread costs over many cases. The less obvious one is that they **play for rules, not outcomes.** A one-shotter wants this case resolved, so they take a settlement. A repeat player will settle the cases it might lose, so no bad precedent is ever made, and fight the ones it can win, so good precedent accumulates. Over decades, even if every individual judge is perfectly fair, the body of law drifts towards the interests of whoever litigates most often.

So it's not only that the rich can afford to use the law. They get more say over what the law is.

## Where the price shows up

**Criminal law.** In US federal courts in 2018, about 90% of defendants pleaded guilty and 2% went to trial, according to a Pew analysis of court data. The rest had their cases dismissed. Defendants who go to trial and lose usually get much longer sentences than the plea offer (the "trial penalty"), and a public defender with hundreds of open cases can't try many of them. The right to a trial is formally universal. Exercising it is a gamble most defendants can't afford.

**Tax.** In 2019 ProPublica found that the IRS was auditing people claiming the Earned Income Tax Credit, mostly households earning under twenty thousand dollars, at about the same rate as the top 1% of earners (around 1.4% and 1.6%). The IRS's explanation was candid. Auditing the poor is cheap and mostly automated. Auditing the rich requires skilled examiners, and it had lost many of them. The rich were audited less partly because they were *harder to audit*, which is the same pattern in reverse: the cost of enforcing the law is higher against people who can afford good advisers.

**Corporations.** In 2012 HSBC admitted to laundering money for drug cartels and sanctioned countries, and paid about 1.9 billion dollars in a deferred prosecution agreement. Nobody was prosecuted. The next year the US Attorney General, Eric Holder, told the Senate that the size of some institutions made it hard to prosecute them without risking wider economic harm. The phrase that stuck was "too big to jail."

**Contracts.** In *AT&T Mobility v. Concepcion* (2011) and *Epic Systems v. Lewis* (2018), the US Supreme Court upheld contract clauses that send disputes to individual arbitration and forbid class actions. A claim worth thirty dollars isn't worth bringing alone. Bundled with a million others it is, and these clauses unbundle it. A company can now write the main collective remedy out of its contracts.

**The law as a weapon.** A SLAPP (strategic lawsuit against public participation) is a lawsuit brought to cost a critic money, not to win. The plaintiff can afford to lose; the defendant can't afford to fight. Many US states, and now the EU, have passed anti-SLAPP laws, which is itself an admission that the cost of a legal process can be used as a punishment.

## What has helped

None of this is new, so there's a long record of attempted fixes. Roughly, they either make protection automatic or make invoking it cheaper.

**Make it automatic.**
- Statutory voidness: unfair terms that are simply invalid, with no need for anyone to challenge them.
- Day fines, which scale the price to the person paying it.
- Defaults built into systems: payroll that can't pay below the minimum wage, deposits held by a third-party scheme rather than by the landlord.

**Make invoking it cheaper.**
- Legal aid. Germany has state-funded advice and court-cost assistance (*Beratungshilfe*, *Prozesskostenhilfe*), and legal-expenses insurance (*Rechtsschutzversicherung*) is unusually common there.
- Public bodies that read the contract for you, like Brazil's PROCON or Germany's consumer centres (*Verbraucherzentralen*). These can bring cases on behalf of everyone affected.
- Small-claims courts with low fees and no lawyers.
- Class actions, which bundle small claims into one worth bringing.
- Contingency fees, where the lawyer is paid only from what they win.

**Pay someone else to invoke it.** This is litigation finance, which I've written about [before](/capitalism-capitalism/) as a market answer to this problem: investors fund cases they think will win, for a share of the proceeds. It does help claimants who couldn't otherwise afford to sue a large company.

But it has the same shape as the problem. Funders choose cases by expected value. A large claim against a solvent defendant is attractive. Burford Capital's best-known case was a claim against Argentina over the nationalisation of the oil company YPF; it won a 16.1-billion-dollar judgment in 2023, which an appeals court overturned in 2026. A tenant's withheld deposit, or a kettle that stops working after thirty days, will never be worth funding, however clear the right. Litigation finance extends the law to claims that are big. The small ones, which is where most people's legal problems live, are still invoked by nobody.

## So does the rule of law exist?

I don't think the answer is no; the rule of law isn't fictional. Courts are mostly honest, and much of the law does apply to everyone: you can't simply buy your way out of a murder charge. The point is that it doesn't exist as a single thing that is either present or absent. I'd rather say that **each right has a cost of invoking it, and the rule of law exists for the people who can pay that cost.** For automatic protections, the cost is roughly zero, and the law really is equal. For invoked ones, it's equal only in the books.

That suggests a simple test for any new protection: does it apply itself, or does someone have to claim it? Each time, it's worth asking whether the second kind could be made into the first.

It also suggests why AI lawyers could matter so much, in either direction. If everyone had a capable bot that knew their rights, noticed when they applied, met the deadlines and filed the forms for free, the cost of invoking a right would collapse. That could be the biggest equaliser since legal aid. Or the cost could collapse for everyone at once, so that a free bot is up against a better-paid one in every transaction, and the gap just moves from *whether* you have a lawyer to *how good* your lawyer is. My guess is that it depends on the distinction above. Where protections are automatic, cheap bots help. Where outcomes are contests between two advocates, whoever pays more still wins.

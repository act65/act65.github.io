The conference put her on last before lunch, which is the slot they give you when they want people to stay in the room. Six hundred founders, most of them under thirty-five, and a Q&A that ran long because it always ran long.

A man in the second row asked who had taken a chance on her.

"Nobody," Sarah said, and waited for the laugh, because there was always a laugh.

"That's not a boast, it's the record. I asked one person for help in my life. I was nineteen, I needed three thousand dollars for a deposit on a workshop unit in Everett, and I asked my uncle Gerry." She did the voice. "*Sarah, I don't have it. And if I had it, I'm not sure I'd give it to you.*"

Bigger laugh.

"And he was right. I'd have taken it and I'd have learned nothing." She let that sit. "People want there to be somebody standing behind me. A mentor, a fund, a name you'd recognise. There isn't one. There never was. I made this."

She looked out at them.

"I only tell you because half of you are waiting for permission from someone who is never going to give it, and you should stop."

Somebody clipped the last ninety seconds. It has been up for six years.

***

In December a supplier sent her a case of Barolo with a card that read *For a great year*. She had it couriered back the same afternoon and put the contract out to tender in January.

In February, in the last week of the Chengsen merger, a junior partner named Mark Oyelaran caught her in the corridor with a one-page proposal. A tech-education program in the two towns where the acquisition would close a plant.

"Whose budget?"

"I thought maybe integration."

"Integration's committed to the day."

"Right." He looked at the page. "I could probably—"

"Bring me a number and I'll look at it."

He didn't bring a number. She didn't think about it again.

***

Three weeks later she was fourteen slides into the Chengsen board presentation when she noticed a dark coin of blood on her cuff. She had not felt it start. She tipped her head forward slightly, kept the clicker moving, and finished the deck.

In the bathroom afterwards she stood over the basin for twenty minutes, then forty, pinching the bridge of her nose, watching the water go pink and clear and pink again. She drove herself to urgent care. She would not drive again until August.

A resident found the rash — not a rash, a scatter of red pinpoints across both shins, like cayenne under the skin, that didn't blanch when he pressed a glass to it. Her platelet count was four. The lower bound of normal is a hundred and fifty.

They admitted her that night. Someone took her razor out of her overnight bag. Someone else took the floss.

***

She solved it the way she solved things.

The referral pathway put the hematology consult at six weeks; she had Dr. Ramirez by Thursday. She flew a second opinion in from Houston. She paid a medical librarian four hundred an hour to pull and rank every paper on acquired marrow failure published since 2015, then read them herself anyway, and by the third week the residents were saying *as you'll have seen* before they explained anything. She took the private room and the room next to it as an office.

Her body had stopped making megakaryocytes — the cells that shed platelets — because her immune system had begun destroying them for no reason anyone could name. There was no cure to buy. But there were nine things that helped, and she bought all nine, and in March she endowed a fellowship at a hematology institute in Boston, a sum she absorbed without restructuring anything, and slept eight hours for the first time since December.

They put her name on a wall. She has never seen it.

Twice a week a bag of platelets went into her arm and she cleared her inbox underneath it.

***

The statement came in April. Eleven pages. She went through it with a ruler, because that is what she had done with every statement since she was twenty-two.

Line 340. *PLATELETS PHERESIS LEUKOREDUCED, 1 unit — $612.00.* It appeared, with variations, ninety-one times.

She called billing.

"I want the breakdown on the pheresis line."

"That's the standard charge."

"Standard for what. What am I buying."

Keyboard. "Collection kit, infectious disease screen, HLA typing, bacterial culture, apheresis machine time, storage, transport, crossmatch, and the nursing."

"And the platelets."

A pause. "The platelets are donated, ma'am."

"I understand that. I'm asking what part of the six hundred and twelve reaches the person they came out of."

"None of it. They're volunteers."

Sarah had the ruler flat under line 340.

"Then who do I pay?"

"Ma'am, your balance is on page one."

***

In late April she stopped responding. The counts went in and came straight back out. Enough foreign platelets had gone through her that her immune system had learned the common type and started destroying that too. Anti-HPA-1a. From now on she needed platelets from donors who lacked the antigen.

"How many people is that?"

"Two percent are HPA-1a negative," Ramirez said. "Of those — typed, on the registry, near a collection center, healthy this week, and willing to actually come in." She turned her hand over. "For you, right now, four. The best match's in Portland."

Sarah wrote *4* on the back of a parking receipt and put it behind her cards.

Then one Friday in July the bag didn't come.

"There's a delay," the nurse said. "They'll call."

Monday, nothing. Tuesday the pinpoints came back across her shins and the insides of her forearms, and she caught herself running her tongue along her gums and tasting iron. Wednesday morning she bled from the nose for two hours, sitting on the edge of the bathtub with a towel, counting the intervals between changing it, shaking, unable to work out whether the shaking was the blood loss.

She got through to a woman named Deb. Transfusion coordinator. A landline, a laminated list, and Sarah had never heard of her.

"Buy them from Denver."

"There's nothing to buy. Platelets are five days from collection. Realistically I've got three."

"Freeze them."

"Doesn't work. Red cells I can bank six weeks, plasma a year. Platelets, no." A drawer opened and shut on the other end. "Everything you've ever had was inside somebody that same week."

"So what happens on a Tuesday?"

"I call Ron. He drives down." A pause, and then a different voice. "I shouldn't have said that."

He'd had a chest infection that ran longer than expected. The second match was on a work trip. The third was over the annual limit. For nine days there had been no mechanism at all, only weather.

***

She had counsel draft it inside a fortnight, because a nine-day gap is a supply problem and she had solved a great many supply problems.

Retain all four, individually, on annual availability agreements. A retainer that would make it worth structuring a year around, plus a per-collection fee, plus travel. She had modelled it against what she would pay to hold a component line open in Shenzhen and it came out cheaper.

"You can't," Ramirez said.

"They're not employees. It's an availability payment."

"You can't pay donors." Ramirez didn't look up from what she was writing. "It's not a technicality, it's the whole architecture. The moment you can, the platelets start coming from people who need the money."

"Then I'll pay well."

"Then they'll come from people who need money *badly*." She capped the pen. "People who need money badly do not tell you about their hepatitis. That's not a slur, it's a finding. The commercial supply used to be measurably more dangerous than the volunteer one. Not marginally."

Sarah sat with that longer than Ramirez expected.

"And there's the other end of it," Ramirez said. "If you can buy platelets you can buy marrow, and if you can buy marrow you can buy a kidney, and then there's a man in Karachi with one kidney and a man in Zurich with two. The rule isn't there to inconvenience you. It's there because of where the alternative goes."

"So I wait my turn."

"You wait your turn."

She funded the recruitment drive instead. Ten thousand people typed across four states, and the center took the money gratefully and told her, in writing, that it would be eighteen months before any of it produced a matched donor.

She signed it anyway, and it did not help her, and somewhere in the second year it presumably helped somebody else, and she has never been told who.

***

The question she could not get past was not why the supply had failed. It was how something this thin was the best that eight billion people had managed.

She opened a spreadsheet on the Thursday. Three columns, because three columns was how she had bought four companies. *What's in the bag. Who worked it out. What they got for it.*

She started with the anticoagulant, because without it nothing else is possible: blood clots in the line within minutes and you are back to arm-to-arm in a single room, hoping. Sodium citrate, 1915. Richard Lewisohn at Mount Sinai spent two years finding the concentration that would stop the clot without poisoning the patient, published it, and watched almost nobody use it for another two. It is also why a donor's lips go numb around the ninety-minute mark.

She had assumed the five days was a regulation. It isn't. Until 1969, platelets were refrigerated like everything else in medicine, and they came out useless, and nobody could say why. Two researchers named Murphy and Gardner left them at room temperature — the wrong thing, the thing you'd be written up for — and the cells lived. Five days is not a policy. It's a property of the cell that somebody had to go and find.

Then she started on the third column, and this is where she got into difficulty, because she had built it to hold money and she could not find any.

She had a paralegal run it the way they ran a target: estate, heirs, patents, assignees, licensing entities, foundations, anything with a name on it. Day and a half. About eleven hundred dollars.

Carl Walter came back clean and complete. The plastic blood bag, 1950 — which sounds like packaging and is the whole of it, because glass bottles cannot be spun into layers, so before the bag there is no such thing as a platelet concentrate, there is only whole blood, all of it, all at once. Patent, assignment, a company, a division of a larger company, revenue in the billions. Sarah read that page twice with something close to relief. It was the only sheet in the file that behaved.

Landsteiner she had assumed would be similar, because he is the one with his name in the textbooks. He sorted the blood of five men in his own Vienna laboratory into three groups in 1901 and published it as a footnote to a paper about something else, and for fifteen years almost nobody transfused on the basis of it. He took the prize in 1930, thirty years late. By every account she could find he was a private, difficult man who did not much enjoy company and cared about priority more than was good for him. In 1943 he had a heart attack at his bench and died two days after. He had no interest whatever in what she was trying to give him.

Percy Oliver was where it stopped being a spreadsheet.

In 1921 a hospital rang the Camberwell division of the British Red Cross wanting a donor. The man who took the call was a local council clerk, and afterwards he started keeping a list of people who would come in for nothing — typed, contactable, willing. He ran it out of his house. His wife answered the telephone. He did it for the rest of his working life and was never paid a shilling for any of it, and it is the reason the word *donor* means what it means in English instead of meaning seller.

The paralegal found no patents, no assignees, no estate of record worth the search.

Sarah looked at that for a while, and then thought about a laminated list in an office she had never been to, and a woman saying *I call Ron*.

***

By October the spreadsheet had a hundred and forty rows and she had stopped keeping them in order.

Not all of it was noble and she stopped expecting it to be. There were priority fights that ran for decades and ruined friendships. There were people who got the thing right and were insufferable about it, and people who got it wrong and defended it for twenty years out of vanity. In the seventies and eighties, commercial plasma pooled from paid donors carried hepatitis and then HIV into hemophiliacs across most of the developed world, and thousands of them died of a product somebody sold at a margin. She put that in too. It belonged in the same column as the rest.

Emil Freireich, at the National Cancer Institute in the early sixties, noticed the children on his leukemia ward were not dying of leukemia. They were dying of bleeding. He was told that transfusing platelets was pointless, probably unethical, and beneath the institution — and he pushed it through anyway, and he was right, and he was also, by the accounts of people who trained under him, a bully who made grown physicians cry in corridors.

Both of those are in her arm on a Tuesday.

The machine took a day longer to trace. The apheresis separator — the thing that takes Ron apart for ninety minutes and gives back everything except the platelets — was built in the mid-sixties by an engineer named George Judson, whom IBM sent to work with Freireich.

Judson's son had leukemia.

He built the machine. His son died.

The patents belong to the company. There is no estate of record, no foundation, no licensing entity, nothing to send anything to. What came back was four paragraphs in a local paper. *Survived by. Retired to. Enjoyed.*

Sarah got as far as the third column and left the cell empty.

***

She went back into the paper for the drug that had turned her around, into the supplementary appendix, which is where you go when you have run out of things to buy. Table S4, treatment arm, a row near the bottom. *Death — 5.* There is a protocol number, a set of site codes, and a line thanking the patients and their families. Their consent forms would have said, in the plain-language section, that the study was unlikely to benefit them personally.

The third column, in the end, said: *nothing. Nothing. A prize, thirty years late. Billions. Nothing, and then dismissed. He was told to stop. His son died. Five of them died and the appendix has the number.*

There was no total. Half the rows had no counterparty and the other half didn't want one.

She was, as far as she could tell from ninety-one bags and eleven pages, close to the first person in the whole chain for whom the thing had simply worked, on a Tuesday, while she answered email.

***

In December she went to donate.

She had it planned. Get typed, and if she was HPA-1a negative she'd be on the registry by Christmas, and if she wasn't she'd give platelets like anyone else, every fortnight, indefinitely.

The screening nurse got quieter going down the history. Autoimmune marrow failure. Ongoing immunosuppression. Ninety-one units of somebody else's platelets. She came back with a form and an apologetic mouth.

"It's the medical director's call."

The call was no.

Sarah appealed it. She wrote four pages with citations and she was not wrong about the literature, and it made no difference, because the decision was a judgement made by a person and there was nothing in it to defeat.

***

In March they asked her back to the conference. Bigger room. She had the deck open on the plane and cut one slide, and nobody in the office noticed, because it had never had anything on it but a photograph of the unit in Everett.

Eleven minutes in, a woman near the front asked who had taken a chance on her.

Sarah had the receipt in her wallet with *4* on the back of it. She had not been able to make herself throw it out.

"A man in Oregon," she said. "And an engineer at IBM."

She let the pause go on a beat too long, then asked for the next slide, and the room decided it had been a joke.

***

The cohort study had been running since 1974. A hematologist named Evelyn Hayes went door to door in her own suburb over two winters and signed up four hundred and six healthy people, so that there would be something to compare the sick ones against. Quarterly bloods, a questionnaire, indefinitely. Nothing in it for anybody in it. Hayes died in 2003. The dataset is cited across most of the field and the four hundred and six are a number in a methods section.

Sarah read the consent form twice looking for the part where she was useful and did not find one.

The intake room was a portacabin behind the hospital with four chairs and a poster about hydration. The phlebotomist took two tubes, wrote a number on each, and set them in a rack beside a dozen others, all numbered, none named.

"What happens to them?"

"Freezer." He capped the pen. "Someone'll thaw them in ten years. If they're lucky."

On the way out the girl at the desk held out a juice box and a packet of two cookies.

Sarah took it.

---

*A note on what's real.* Sarah, Ramirez, Deb, Ron, Mark, Evelyn Hayes and her cohort are invented, as are the details of Sarah's searches. Everyone else is not.

Richard Lewisohn worked out the citrate concentration in 1915. Karl Landsteiner published the ABO groups in 1901, won the Nobel in 1930, and died in 1943 after a heart attack in his laboratory. Carl Walter's plastic blood bag arrived in 1950 and was commercialised; Scott Murphy and Frank Gardner showed in 1969 that platelets survive at room temperature and not in the cold, which is where the five days comes from. Percy Oliver, a council clerk, started the first voluntary unpaid donor panel in Camberwell in 1921 and ran it for decades. Charles Drew built the wartime plasma program and lost his argument about segregating donated blood. Emil Freireich pushed platelet transfusion through against his institution, and George Judson, the IBM engineer who built the cell separator with him, had a son with leukemia.

Richard Titmuss's *The Gift Relationship* (1970) is behind Ramirez's answer: comparing the British voluntary system with the American commercial one, he found that paying for blood produced a supply that was both scarcer and more dangerous. The contaminated factor concentrates of the seventies and eighties bore him out at a cost of thousands of lives.

In [What money remembers]({{ site.baseurl }}/money-and-reputation/) I said I hadn't seen a good alternative to money as the incentive for getting strangers to do useful things for each other. That deserves to be checked rather than asserted. So this post tries to do the check: list the other answers people have actually tried, and look at what happened when they were tried at scale.

I've tried to be fair. Where I couldn't find out what happened, I've said so.

#### Two jobs, not one

It helps to separate two things money does at once.

1. **Motivating.** Money gives you a reason to do something unpleasant for someone you don't care about.
2. **Directing.** Prices tell you *which* unpleasant thing is most worth doing. More people want bread than want another sculpture of a horse.

Many alternatives handle the first job well. Far fewer handle the second. The second job is the subject of the old socialist calculation debate, which I think is better read as an argument about who computes the prices than about who owns the factories. <!-- TODO: link the "Prices are Lagrange multipliers" draft once it's published --> This post is mostly about the first job, but the second keeps coming back.

#### The candidates

**Kinship and gifts.** Families and small bands run almost entirely without internal prices. Anthropologists have documented elaborate gift economies, such as the Kula ring of the Trobriand Islands, where gifts circulate and create obligations rather than settling them.[^mauss] These work. They also seem to depend on everyone knowing everyone, and on the obligations being remembered. So they are reputation systems, which is the thing money compressed in the first place.

**Honour and status.** People will do a lot for standing in the eyes of others: medals, titles, being well thought of. The potlatch feasts of the Pacific Northwest turned giving things away into a competition for status. This motive clearly scales further than gifts. Soldiers, scientists and Wikipedia editors all respond to it. But status is itself a ledger, kept by a community, and it is often converted into money sooner or later.

**Duty and belief.** Monasteries, religious orders and some volunteer movements have kept people doing hard, useful work for life, without pay, for centuries. My suspicion is that they work partly because of *who joins*. When membership is voluntary, the people inside are the ones the motive already works on. That's a real achievement, but it doesn't obviously apply to everyone else.

**Shared ownership with equal sharing: the kibbutz.** The Israeli kibbutzim are the best-documented large test I know of. For most of the twentieth century they shared income almost equally among members, and at their peak held something over a hundred thousand people. Ran Abramitzky's economic history of them argues that equal sharing held up for decades, and that the main pressure on it was *who left*. Members with the best outside prospects were the most likely to go. From the 1990s, after a financial crisis, many kibbutzim moved to differential pay.[^abramitzky] I read this as a qualified success: equal sharing worked for longer and at a larger scale than I'd have guessed, but it had to keep fighting the exit option.

**Shared ownership, still paid: cooperatives.** Mondragon, a federation of worker cooperatives in the Basque country, employs tens of thousands of people. The workers own the firms and the gap between the highest and lowest pay is capped. But people are still paid in money. This changes *who owns*, not *what motivates*. It's worth noticing because I think much left-wing argument is about this kind of change, and not about replacing money at all.

**Planning.** The Soviet Union is the obvious large-scale attempt to do without markets. However, it did not do without money as an incentive. Workers were paid wages, often piece rates, and given honours ("Hero of Socialist Labour", the Stakhanovite campaigns). What it removed was markets for *capital*: the directing job, not the motivating one. And alongside wages and medals it used the third answer below.

**Coercion.** This is the honest historical answer to "how do you get people to do work they'd rather not do?" Slavery, serfdom, corvée labour and conscription all worked at enormous scale. I include it not as a candidate but because, as far as I can tell, it's what most societies did before markets spread. Any alternative to money ought to be compared with this, not only with an ideal.

**Volunteer production: open source and Wikipedia.** These are, to me, the most striking modern cases. Millions of people have contributed to software and an encyclopedia that billions use, mostly without pay. The motives are a mix of curiosity, craft, status and needing the tool yourself. Two caveats. First, a large share of the work on major open-source projects is now done by people employed to do it. <!-- TODO: find the current Linux Foundation figure rather than gesture at it --> Second, these are goods that cost nothing to copy, and the volunteers can afford to volunteer. I don't know of a volunteer equivalent for collecting rubbish.

**Commons governed by their users.** Elinor Ostrom spent a career documenting communities that managed shared resources well for centuries without either private property or the state: Swiss alpine pastures, Spanish irrigation systems, Japanese village forests.[^ostrom] Her design principles include monitoring by members and graduated sanctions for those who break the rules. That is, again, a community memory of who behaved well, used to decide who gets what.

**Reputation scores run by a state.** China's social credit systems are sometimes held up as a non-money incentive at national scale. As I understand it, the reality is more fragmented than the Western coverage suggests, mostly blacklists for unpaid debts and court orders. Either way, it shows what goes wrong when the reputation ledger is held by an authority that also decides what counts as good behaviour.

#### A summary, roughly

| | motivates? | directs? | largest scale I know of | what it seems to rely on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kinship and gifts | yes | locally | bands, villages | everyone knows everyone |
| Honour and status | yes | weakly | professions, armies | a community keeping score |
| Duty and belief | yes | no | religious orders | self-selected members |
| Kibbutz | yes | locally | ~100k people | limiting exit, shared values |
| Cooperatives | yes (money) | yes (prices) | tens of thousands | still money |
| Planning | yes (money, honours, force) | badly | whole countries | still money, plus coercion |
| Coercion | yes | no | whole empires | violence |
| Open source / Wikipedia | yes | weakly | millions | free copying, comfortable volunteers |
| Ostrom commons | yes | locally | regional resources | monitoring and sanctions |

#### What I take from it

Three patterns stand out to me, tentatively.

**The large ones still use money underneath.** The cooperatives, the planned economies and much of open source all pay wages. What they change is ownership, or prices for capital, or who decides. None of them replaces the wage.

**The ones that don't use money are reputation systems.** Gifts, honour, Ostrom's commons, even the kibbutz: each relies on a community remembering who contributed. If [money is compressed reputation]({{ site.baseurl }}/money-and-reputation/), then these aren't really alternatives to money. They are the uncompressed version, and they hit the same limit: memory runs out when the group gets too large for everyone to know each other.

**I may have been arguing against a position few people hold.** Most of the left-wing proposals I found are about who owns things and what they're allowed to do, not about abolishing pay. Looked at that way, "what would replace money as the incentive?" may be a question for a small part of the left, not the whole of it. The bigger argument is about the directing job, and about whether the ledger is measuring the right thing.

That leaves a narrower question than the one I started with. Is there an incentive that scales to strangers, doesn't need coercion, and records something closer to "good done" than money does? Every candidate above either stops at the size of a community or turns back into money. I still haven't found one that doesn't. I'd like to be shown one.

### 2000

Dad's game was Friday nights in our kitchen, for tens and twenties. Uncle Mick, who wasn't my uncle, and Les and Barry from the cash-and-carry where Dad did the night stock. They played with a deck where the ace of clubs had gone missing years ago and been replaced by a joker with ACE written on it in biro, and nobody ever thought to buy a new one.

I was allowed to watch from the stairs if I didn't talk. The week I turned fourteen I was allowed to play one hand, with fifty pence of my own.

I got a seven and a two. I knew enough to know that was the worst hand there is, because Dad had told me. But it was my one hand and I'd been waiting all evening, so when Les bet, I pushed the fifty pence in. The first three cards came seven, seven, king. Les had a king. He looked at the last card for a long time, shaking his head, while I scraped the coins over to my side of the table.

"Well," said Les.

Dad didn't say well done. He waited until the others had gone and he was rinsing the glasses, and then he said, "You know that was a bad call."

"I won."

"You won. Doesn't make it good." He put a glass upside down on the drainer. "You can't do anything about the cards, Neil. All you get to decide is whether you put your money in good. Win with a bad call, you've learned nothing. Lose with a good one, you've lost nothing. Not really."

I said I understood. I remember the coins more, though. Three pound twenty in tens and twenties, heavier than it should have been.

That was the spring of the Pokémon cards. Everyone at school had them and nobody could get any more. The newsagent on Station Road had a waiting list in the back of a diary. Dad could get a box of thirty-six packs at trade with his staff card, for less than the newsagent paid. I asked him if he would. He looked at me for a while and said if I paid him back out of my birthday money he'd get me two.

I sold the packs at school for three pounds each, which was more than the shop price, but the shop didn't have any. Then I worked out that the rare cards were worth more on their own, and started opening the packs and selling cards one at a time. A shiny Charizard went for twenty-five pounds to a Year Nine whose parents were splitting up. By May I had three hundred and forty pounds in a Quality Street tin under my bed, and I'd paid Dad back.

On a Friday in May, Gary Pratt's older brother offered me a hundred and twenty for everything I had left, to sell on at the market in town. I wanted Tony Hawk's Pro Skater. I said yes.

On the Monday, Mr Fuller announced in assembly that Pokémon cards were banned from school premises with immediate effect, after a fight in the Year Seven toilets. Gary's brother had three hundred cards and a market stall full of people selling the same thing.

I was fourteen. I had four hundred and sixty pounds and a PlayStation game, and I'd learned something about myself that I have never stopped knowing: I can feel when a thing is about to turn.

### 2005

At university I lived on a corridor with eleven other people, and in the first week I noticed that all of them were paying full price for textbooks the year above had just finished with. The year above sold their books back to the campus bookshop for a quarter of what they'd paid, and the bookshop sold them on again for three-quarters. Somebody was making half the price of every book in the building, and it wasn't us.

That was the whole idea, and it was a good one. I still think so.

I registered textswap.co.uk that night. Textswap.com was taken, so I got textswapuk.com as well. I spent most of the first term on the logo. People think that's vanity, but you only get one go at a brand. You can change your code every week, but you can't change what people first thought of you. I did it in green, and then I did it in blue. I printed four hundred flyers with the blue one before I'd decided how the site would work, because the print shop did a deal on four hundred.

Priyesh, from the end of the corridor, did Computer Science. He'd seen the flyers. He said he could build the site in about a fortnight, since it was basically a list with a search box, and did I want to go halves.

I said I'd been thinking more like eighty-twenty, given that it was my idea and my brand and I'd already paid for the domains and the flyers. He said fair enough and went back to his room. He didn't sound annoyed at all.

I found a web designer in Leeds on a forum who quoted nine hundred pounds, which went on the student overdraft. He sent me a homepage in November. I sent it back, because he'd used the green logo.

In January, Priyesh started a Facebook group called *Sell Your Books (Year Above → Year Below)*. It had no logo. You posted a photo of the book and a price, and people messaged you. By Easter it had three thousand members, and the campus bookshop had put up a sign saying buyback prices were under review.

When I brought it up in the kitchen he said, "Yeah, it was a good idea," and kept stirring his pasta, as if that settled something.

Textswap went live in April, when everybody already had their books. Forty-one people registered. Afterwards I went through it all properly, and the answer was simple. Facebook had reached our campus that year. One year later and everyone would have been on Textswap, because there'd have been nothing else. You can't plan for Facebook.

I got a 2:2. I'd put a lot of hours into Textswap.

### 2013

After university I sold mortgages for a broker above a Greggs, and I was good at it. People liked me. I'm easy to talk to and I believe what I'm saying, and you can't fake that across a desk. I bought a flat. I met Claire at a work thing. She was in compliance, and she laughed in the right places and then asked me a question I couldn't answer about my own flat, and I liked her for both.

I played online most nights after she'd gone to bed. Small stakes. I kept the results in tracking software that saved every hand, because that's what serious players do.

Dad died the winter before Brick. In the hospice he was very thin and mostly wanted to talk about the game, which Mick had kept going in his own kitchen. He asked how I was getting on online. I said I was down a bit on the year, running bad. He nodded like he knew what that meant. Then he said, "You always put your money in, I'll say that for you."

I've thought about it a lot, and I think it was a compliment.

The idea for Brick came on a train when my phone died outside Peterborough. A phone case with a battery in the back, slim enough that you wouldn't notice it. I called it Brick, which was a joke about old phones, and also about how solid it was. I drew it on the back of my ticket.

I checked. There was one already, an American one, but it was thick and nobody here had heard of it, which as far as I could see meant the market was proven and the field was open.

I filed a provisional patent myself, online, for about a hundred and fifty pounds, so I was protected. I found a factory in Shenzhen that would make them for eleven pounds a unit on an order of two thousand. They'd sell for twenty-nine. Eighteen pounds a case. I made a spreadsheet: two thousand cases, thirty-six thousand pounds; ten thousand in year two. I asked everyone at five-a-side, and every one of them said they'd buy one. Two of them said they'd buy two.

Claire asked where the twenty-two thousand for the first order was coming from. I said Kickstarter would pay for the order, that was the point of Kickstarter, and the money for the video and the samples could come from Mum, who had offered. That was true. At the funeral she'd held my hand and said, "If you ever need anything, love." She lent me fifteen thousand. I found out a long time afterwards that she'd taken it out against the house.

I handed in my notice the day the Kickstarter went live, because investors want to see you're all in.

To get it moving I put on an early-bird tier. The first fifteen hundred backers got a Brick for nineteen pounds, posted free, anywhere in the world. You give something away at the start. Everyone knows that.

It went mad. We hit the target in four days. An American tech blog picked it up, then a German one. Claire refreshed it on her phone under the table at her sister's birthday and kept showing me under the tablecloth. Strangers in Ohio were writing *take my money* in the comments. At the end of thirty days there were 2,380 backers and fifty-one thousand pounds, three hundred and forty percent of the target. Claire bought champagne. Mum came round and cried. I took a photo of the total on my laptop with the two of them either side of it, and for years it was the picture on my phone.

Then I sat down to work out the postage.

Two-thirds of the backers were abroad, and nearly all of them had taken the early bird. Royal Mail had changed the international rates in the spring, and a tracked small parcel to America was now eleven pounds forty. Kickstarter and the card fees took eight percent off the top. When I told the factory it would be 2,500 cases, not 2,000, they said that was a different production run and the price was now twelve pounds twenty. They also said the battery needed a safety certificate before it could go on a plane at all, and that was three thousand pounds more.

The first time, I did it on a sheet of A4 at the kitchen table and assumed I'd made a mistake. One early bird to America: nineteen pounds, less one fifty-two in fees, less twelve twenty for the case, less eleven forty to post it. Minus six pounds twelve. Every early bird abroad cost me six pounds twelve to send. The more people loved Brick, the more it cost.

The cases went out seven months late. The comments stopped saying *take my money*. I put the shortfall on two credit cards and the certificate on a third. The provisional patent ran out after twelve months. The full one would have cost four thousand pounds, and I didn't have four thousand pounds by then. A few months later a Chinese company was selling a case on Amazon that looked exactly like Brick, for sixteen pounds, with free delivery.

I went back to mortgages, at a different broker. I set up a standing order to Mum for a hundred pounds a month. I've never missed one.

When people ask about Brick, I tell them the truth: it was too successful, too fast. Nobody plans for going viral.

### 2020

In the first lockdown I couldn't sleep, and I started watching physics lectures at two in the morning, and then reading. I read about entropy, the second law, how disorder in a closed system can only ever go up. Then I read that Claude Shannon had found the same equation, the exact same one, for information. Two subjects, one law.

I sat in the spare room with the lamp off and I could feel it, the way I'd felt the cards turning in Year Nine.

A market is information. Prices are information. When a market gets too orderly, when every day moves the same small amount in the same direction, its entropy is low. The second law says entropy has to go back up. Disorder has to come back. That's a crash, and you can see it coming, because you can measure it.

I taught myself enough Python to do it. Take the daily price changes, sort them into bins by size, count how many land in each bin, and put the counts through Shannon's formula. Low number, the market's too calm. High number, the storm's already been.

I wrote to eleven physics professors. One wrote back.

> Dear Neil,
>
> Thanks for writing, and for the obvious enthusiasm. The second law is about the thermodynamic entropy of an isolated physical system. The Shannon entropy of a histogram of price changes is a different quantity. The formula looks the same, but there's no law saying it has to increase; it can go up or down as it likes. If you want to know whether your measure predicts anything, the honest test is to fix all your choices in advance and then run it on data you haven't looked at yet.
>
> Best of luck with it.
>
> A. Okafor

I read it five or six times. It's the most useful email I've ever had, because it showed me exactly where academia's blind spot was. They'd split it into two subjects in the 1940s and never put it back together. He as good as said himself that the formula was the same.

I took his point about testing seriously. I tried every window from ten days to ninety, and every bin width from a tenth of a percent to four percent, about three thousand combinations, on twenty years of the FTSE. The best was forty-three days with bins of 0.7 percent. It called 2008 three weeks early.

I called it Entropic. The second slide of the deck was about me.

> **Founder.** First venture at fourteen: identified the top of a collectibles boom and exited at peak, three days before a regulatory shutdown. Built and launched a student marketplace. Took a hardware product to 340% of its funding target. Eleven years' experience in financial services.

All of it true.

The FTSE was too slow to show what Entropic could do, so I moved it onto Bitcoin, which moved every day. Claire put in the forty thousand in her ISA. Rob from five-a-side put in twenty-five. I put in my half of what was left of the flat, after we'd moved to a house with a spare room.

From October 2020 it went up seventy percent in seven months.

I'm not going to pretend I didn't enjoy it. A man called Dex, with a podcast about trading psychology, had me on for an hour and a quarter. I had cards printed: *Neil Hollis, Founder & Chief Scientist*. Claire and I went to Portugal for ten days and she read three novels by a pool, and every evening she asked how it was doing, and every evening I showed her. Rob called me Professor. I emailed Dr Okafor a chart. He didn't reply, which I understood. It's a lot to take in.

In May 2022 a coin called TerraUSD, which was supposed to be worth exactly one dollar forever, stopped being worth anything in about three days, and took most of the market with it. Entropic had been reading high, the storm's-been-and-gone signal, and I was in with leverage. It lost eighty-one percent in six weeks.

Nobody saw Terra coming. Nobody.

I told Claire on a Tuesday. Afterwards I drove to the big Tesco and sat in the car park with the engine off until they'd turned the trolley-park lights off too.

Something started up in me then that I'd never let get going before. It was quite calm. It went through the list. Textswap. Brick. Entropic. Mum's fifteen thousand, still five thousand to go at a hundred a month. Claire's forty. Rob, who'd stopped coming to five-a-side. It said: three times now. You were there all three times. Priyesh had a list with a search box. A Chinese factory had your case for sixteen pounds. A professor told you in plain English, and you put it on a slide. It said: maybe it's not the timing. Maybe you're just not—

And I stopped, because that's not how you think about it. Not if you think about it properly.

Nine startups out of ten fail. Everyone knows that. So the chance of one founder failing three times in a row is nine-tenths times nine-tenths times nine-tenths. I did it on the back of a parking ticket: 0.729. Nearly three in four. If you started a thousand companies with a thousand founders who each tried three times, seven hundred and twenty-nine of them would be sitting in a car park somewhere. It would have been strange if it *hadn't* happened.

Three hands isn't a sample. Nobody serious judges a player on three hands.

I sat there a while longer, but it was a different kind of sitting. Then I drove home and made us both tea.

Claire didn't leave. She opened an account in her own name and moved her salary into it, and I understood that. It's what you'd do with a player on a downswing. You don't stop believing in him. You just stop staking him.

### 2026

Sunday night. Claire's gone up.

The tracking software has every hand I've played since 2009, one million one hundred and twelve thousand of them. The graph is three lines.

The green one is what I've actually won and lost: down twenty-one thousand four hundred pounds. The dotted one is what I'd have won if every all-in had gone the way the maths said it should. That's down seventeen thousand one hundred. The gap between them is four thousand three hundred pounds of pure, measured bad luck. It isn't my opinion. It's in the software.

The red line goes down from the top-left corner at about forty-five degrees, all the way, and comes out at minus twenty-six thousand. That's the hands that never got to a showdown, the folds and the blinds. Everyone's red line goes down. The forums are full of it.

I've had an idea. An app that reads your hand histories and shows you where you're losing money, hand by hand, with AI. I've got a million hands to train it on. Leakfinder.com is taken, but leakfinder.ai was fifty-nine pounds. I've done a version of the logo in blue.

Mum's last payment goes out in March.

I logged on and took a seat at a table with five strangers, and posted my blind. The cards came. A seven and a two. I knew what Dad would say. But it had been a long day, and I'd been waiting all evening, and I put my money in.

I got in at twenty past one and put the receipt on the bench, under the power bill, where things go that I'll deal with at the weekend.

Priya wrote her number on the back of it. She had a pen in her coat pocket. She always had a pen.

### 1.

There were five people in my year who just saw it.

Nadia Cross would have her hand up before the lecturer had finished writing the line, and she wasn't showing off, she just wanted to get on to the next bit. I'd still be copying down line three. I sat at the back partly so I could watch, because you could tell who'd got it. The ones who'd got it went still. The rest of us nodded.

I put my hand up a lot, but not like Nadia. I'd say, "Sorry, can I just — why is that step allowed?" and my ears would go hot, every single time, twenty-odd times a semester for three years, and there'd be this little breath out from the rows around me.

Second year, Dr Halloway was doing something with a change of variables and I asked why we could assume the thing was smooth there, and he said "because it is," and then stopped, and looked at the board for about eight seconds, and said, "Actually — hang on."

He went back four lines and rubbed out a whole section and did it again a different way. I have honestly never in my life been happier than I was for the rest of that afternoon. I walked to the bus stop the long way.

I kept notebooks. Black ones, from the campus shop, three-fifty each, and I'd write the number on the cover in silver pen. At the back of each one there was a list headed **things I don't understand yet**, and when I understood one I ruled a line through it. Notebook 11 has *why do fireflies sync* crossed out and *why does the tap water taste different in Dunedin* still open, which is not a serious question, but I wasn't precious about the list.

Wednesday nights I ran the second-year study group in room 4.12. Nobody asked me to, it just sort of accreted, and I brought biscuits because otherwise people drifted off around nine. There was a guy called Ross who'd decided he was going to drop out, and he told me that in the corridor in about week five, and I said give it till the exam, and he said what's the point, and I said because you got the thing about the eigenvalues before I did, which was true, and he looked at me like I'd made it up. He didn't drop out. He's a hydrologist now. He sent me a message when Mum died.

Ana was doing physics and she had a laugh you could hear from two floors up. I could be on the mezzanine and know which corner of the library she was in without looking over the rail.

One night — two in the morning, third year, both of us wrecked — she asked what I actually wanted. Not in a big way. She was eating chips out of the packet with her feet on my chair.

I said, "One thing. One thing that's true, that people use. It doesn't have to be big."

She thought about it and said that was the least ambitious ambitious thing she'd ever heard, and I said I'd take it, and she gave me the chips.

### 2.

She got Zurich in the September. Fully funded, four years, the institute. She came and found me in the lab and she was doing a thing with her hands where she couldn't keep them still.

I said take it before you finish the sentence. I meant it. I helped her with the forms because she was hopeless with forms and I liked doing them, and I remember sitting on her floor with the printouts feeling completely, straightforwardly happy for her, for about four days.

Then there were five months of eleven-hour time differences and calls where one of us was always about to go to sleep. The last one wasn't even a bad call. She said "I'll ring you Sunday" and I said "yep," and we both knew.

The capstone was four of us and a full year. Two weeks before submission I was tidying the repository at about one in the morning and I pulled the raw exports for section three, and the numbers couldn't have come off that instrument. The spacing was wrong. Nothing samples at that interval.

I checked it four times. Then I checked it a fifth time in the specific way you check when you're hoping to find your own mistake.

I went to Nathan on his own, in the carpark, and gave him the chance to pull it himself. I'd rehearsed it. I said we could tell them we'd found a problem in the data and needed to drop that section, and nobody would even ask.

He said, "It's within the error anyway." Then he said, "Don't be a dick about this, Tobe."

So it went to the department. Eleven weeks of meetings, and at the end they voided the project for all four of us, because that's how the regulation was written. Priya lost her year in it too. She has never once brought it up, in twenty-eight years, and there was a stretch where I'd have preferred it if she had.

My honours grade came out a band low. Professor Ellery, who'd been going to write me a reference, wrote me a careful one instead, and I could hear the carefulness in it when I read it.

Then the scholarship. They rang to tell me, which was decent of them, and the woman said, "You were next. I'm sorry, you were literally next."

I did nothing much for about three weeks. Slept till eleven. Told Mum on the phone that I was flat out. I had a thought that kept arriving at around two in the afternoon, which was that maybe I wasn't actually any good, and that Nadia Cross would have been in Zurich and would have had the scholarship and would not have made an eleven-week formal complaint out of a project she could have just quietly fixed.

Then I got a technician's job at the soils lab, because you can't stay in bed forever and because it had an instrument in it I wanted to get my hands on. Fourteen months, terrible money, and I got one small honest paper out of it about a sampling artefact. Three people read it. Possibly two.

Dad printed it out and put it on the fridge with a magnet shaped like a tomato. It was still there when he died.

And it worked — the paper and the fourteen months got me into Meikle's group, on the work rather than the transcript. I was twenty-five and about eighteen months behind where I'd meant to be, and I had climbed the whole way back on my own, and I knew it, and I was insufferable about it for approximately a week.

### 3.

The virus was four days. A fever and a cough that hung about, and I was pleased with myself for going back on the Monday.

Six weeks after that I was stopping on the landing between the second and third floor. Not to catch my breath — to sit down.

Twelve weeks after that I was sleeping eleven hours and waking up like I'd done a shift.

I built a spreadsheet. Sleep, resting heart rate, what I'd eaten, steps, and a column called NEXT DAY where I gave the following day a score out of ten. Four months in I could tell you that anything over about two thousand two hundred steps cost me the next day and a half, reliably, and that it didn't matter how the two thousand two hundred were distributed. None of the three doctors I saw knew that. I printed it for the second one and he looked at it for a while and said, "You're clearly under a lot of stress."

I sat in the car afterwards and thought: he thinks I'm doing this. He thinks I'm doing this to myself.

And the trouble is that once that's in your head, you're the one who has to prove it isn't true, at two in the morning, with no evidence except a spreadsheet you made yourself.

The third doctor was a woman called Dr Anand who said, "I don't know what this is. Nobody does. Most people do get better, and I can't tell you when." I could have kissed her.

It took a year. The leave was unpaid because there isn't another kind. Three years of saving went in nine months, and then I rang Dad, which took me two days to work up to, and he said "how much" before I'd finished the sentence. I paid it back over the following four years and he didn't want it and I sat in his kitchen and made him take the last of it in cash.

I came out of it at about ninety per cent. Ninety per cent is fine for nearly everything.

The first morning I got up to the third floor without stopping I stood outside the door of the lab for a minute before I went in, because I didn't want anyone to see my face.

### 4.

The idea came on the 14 bus.

I'd been staring at a drift curve for two weeks and I was not thinking about it, I was thinking about whether I had milk, and then I thought: it isn't the sensor. It's the reference. You could take the whole error out if you flipped the order of the two measurements and let the reference float.

I nearly went past my stop. I got off and walked back and I remember standing on the footpath outside the dairy trying to break it, and not being able to.

Then I checked it for four months before I told anybody. That's not caution, that's superstition — I had a feeling that if I said it out loud too early it'd turn out to be nothing.

It wasn't nothing. Meikle came into the lab on a Thursday with the plots I'd sent him printed out and folded in his hand, and he said, in front of Ravi and the two masters students, "This is better than mine. Toby, this is genuinely better than anything I've done on this."

I don't think I have ever liked anyone as much as I liked him for about six months.

The studentship was co-funded. I knew that; it was in the folder of forms on the first morning and I read them, all of them, at twenty-five, sitting on the floor of my flat, because I read things. Intellectual property arising was assigned. Publication was subject to a review period, which could be extended.

The review period was extended in the March. Then again in the July. Meikle kept saying "it's a process thing, it's a process thing."

In the November we had a meeting in his office and there was a woman there from the company who I'd never met, and she was perfectly pleasant, and she explained that the method was now core to a product line and could not appear in the literature, and that my thesis would need to be structured around it.

I said, "Around it."

She said, "You've got plenty of other material."

I did have plenty of other material. The thesis I wrote was competent and hollow and was about the second-best thing I have ever thought of.

I read the agreement again at my kitchen table with a highlighter, twice, once at two in the morning and once properly with coffee, and I was right about what it said and it didn't help. I built a folder for the postgraduate ombudsman with tabs and a contents page. Fourteen months. Nobody was rude to me at any point in the entire fourteen months, and I want to say that, because it's true and because it's the part that made it unbearable — everyone I dealt with was decent and sorry and helpless.

The finding came as a paragraph on my phone while I was in a queue at the supermarket. The university had followed its policies correctly.

### 5.

That winter was the worst one.

It started with not answering the phone. Just letting it go, and then the missed call sitting there getting older, and every hour it aged the harder it was to ring back, until ringing back would have required an explanation I didn't have.

There was a postdoc offer. A good one, in Melbourne, from a man who'd read my one small paper about the sampling artefact and remembered it. Deadline the 30th. I looked at that email on the 12th and thought, I'll do it tomorrow when I'm sharper. I looked at it on the 19th. On the 26th I opened it and read it four times and closed it. On the 1st it had gone past and something in me went slack with relief, and then about ten minutes later I understood what I'd done and I put my head on the table.

Here is what it was like in there.

It wasn't sadness. It was a voice, and the voice was reasonable, and it had the whole file.

It would say: four things have gone wrong and you were present at all four. Ana, the capstone, the year off, Meikle. What's the common factor. It would say: Nadia Cross runs a group at Imperial. Ross is a hydrologist. Nathan, who faked the data, is a senior scientist at a Crown institute, and has a house. It would say: you weren't cheated, you were just slower, and slower people get overtaken, and you've dressed it up as bad luck for fifteen years because the alternative is worse. It would say: you're thirty-one and you have a hollow thesis, no money, no partner, and a folder with tabs in it.

And then it would do the thing it did best, which was to be kind for a second, so I'd trust it. It would say: nobody's blaming you. Nobody thinks you should have done better. That's the point. Nobody expects anything from you now. You could stop. You could just stop.

And that felt so good that I'd have to get up and walk around.

I looked at jobs at Dad's mate's engineering firm. Ordering, stock, the counter. I sat with that page open for about an hour and I felt this enormous flooding relief, like getting into a hot bath, and underneath the relief something was screaming, and I couldn't hear what it was saying, and I closed the laptop.

There's about six weeks in the middle of it I can't reconstruct. I know I ate, because of the containers.

Dev turned up on a Tuesday.

I hadn't answered three of his messages. He knocked, and when I opened the door he held up a bag and said, "Butter chicken, and I got the garlic naan, and I'm not staying long," and then he stayed four hours and we watched most of a snooker tournament and he did not ask me how I was doing once.

He came the next Tuesday. And the one after that. Sixteen Tuesdays, I worked out later. He never asked. He'd talk about his sister's renovation and the snooker and a dispute he was having with a neighbour about a hedge, and about three weeks in I started talking back, and about six weeks in I said something funny and heard myself laugh and it sounded like someone else in the room.

Dr Anand had moved practices but the new GP was good. Second thing she tried worked. It took five weeks to come on and when it did it was like someone had turned the gain down on the voice — it was still there, saying the same things, but from further away, and you could get on with the dishes over the top of it.

And then it was a Saturday morning in the September and I was doing the dishes, and I thought: hang on, why does the drift reverse when you cool the room from one side?

I stood there with my hands in the water for I don't know how long. Then I dried them on my jeans and went and found notebook 29 and wrote it in the back, and I remember looking at my own handwriting doing it and thinking, oh — there you are.

I rang Priya that afternoon. I had a whole thing sketched out by the Tuesday. I was thirty-two and eight years behind everyone I'd started with, and I had a laptop, a bad back, an idea, and I genuinely, honestly believed I was going to be fine.

### 6.

We called it Northline because we'd both spent a summer on the same stretch of road and neither of us could think of anything better.

Priya did the first year and then got an offer in another city that she'd have been mad to turn down, and we did the paperwork over a weekend and stayed friends, and then over about six years we became the kind of friends who mean to call.

The first unit went out in the March. I drove it to the customer myself, four hours each way, because I wanted to see it go in. The technician who installed it was called Wiremu and he ran the calibration and looked at the residuals and said "huh," and then he said it again, and then he called someone over.

Four years later there were eleven of us and the thing was in about ninety labs.

I hired Steph when she'd been out of work five years. Her CV had a hole in it you could drive a bus through and she apologised for it in the interview before I'd asked, and I said I didn't need to know, and I could see from the two pages of notes she'd made about our product that she'd already found a bug in it. She was the best hire I ever made. She's at a medical device company now. She still sends me a message at Christmas and I still send one back, which takes me about forty minutes to write.

I was not a natural at running a company. I read about it and asked people and got better, which is the only method I've ever had for anything, and by the fourth year I was decent at it.

It went in five months.

The instrument-grant scheme was discontinued at the budget with a twelve-month wind-down and it reached me as a paragraph in a PDF that a customer forwarded me on a Thursday morning with the subject line "have you seen this." Forty per cent of our pipeline was people spending that money.

The bridge round had been agreed on a call. It was not signed. I have the email where he says "we're in, just working through committee."

Then the bank called in the facility, which I had personally guaranteed at thirty-four, because at thirty-four I had been sure.

I told everyone in the workshop on a Wednesday afternoon because I couldn't do it in the meeting room, there wasn't space for eleven. I got about a third of the way through and had to stop and Steph said, "Take your time, Tobe."

The accountant told me not to pay the two months' notice. He said it very carefully, twice, so that it would be clear afterwards that he'd said it. He was right. Paying it is what turned an insolvency into a personal bankruptcy.

I'd do it again. I've been over it maybe a thousand times and I'd do it again.

Afterwards there was a period of about a year and a half where the voice came back and it had new material.

It would say: eleven people. It would say: Steph took your call in the car and had to pull over. It would say: you were told about the guarantee, in writing, by a lawyer you paid, and you signed it because you liked the feeling of being the kind of man who signs it. It would say: it wasn't the budget. The budget's what you tell people. You should have had two customers' worth of runway and a signed term sheet and you had neither, and everyone competent knows that, and they've been nice to you about it.

And then, the kind bit: you're forty. You've had a go. Nobody would think less of you.

I lived in a room in a house with three people in their twenties for two years. I did contract work. I applied for things — I've still got the list, forty-one applications over three years — and I got close twice, once to a research engineer job I'd have loved, where they went with someone internal and the manager rang me himself to say so.

But I kept looking. That's the bit I'd want anyone to know about those three years. I never stopped looking.

### 7.

Mum was diagnosed in the November and died fourteen months later, and I moved into the front room for eleven of those months.

I did it properly. I had a folder — of course I had a folder — with the medications and the appointment dates and the questions to ask, and I built the ramp myself off the back door and got it signed off by the council, and the inspector said it was better than the ones they get from builders.

There was an afternoon near the end when she was lucid and we were watching the birds on the fence and she said, out of nowhere, "You know you were the one I never had to worry about." I said, "Yeah." I have thought about that sentence approximately every week since.

Dev died nineteen months after her. Cardiac, forty-four, on a Sunday, no warning of any kind.

In the last year of Northline I'd stopped replying to him properly. Not on purpose. I was drowning and he was the one person who'd forgive it. There are two messages of his I never answered at all. One says *no worries mate, next week then.* The other one says *found that bike track I was on about, it's not as steep as I made out. free sat?*

They're still on the phone. I've never deleted them and I've never opened that thread again.

I did the reading at the funeral and I did it well, and afterwards I was outside on the footpath holding a paper cup of tea and I could not work out what the next thing was. Not in a big way. Literally: whether you drink the tea, or find a bin, or go to the car. I stood there for a while and someone put a hand on my back and moved me along.

I got up from that one too. It took two years and it was greyer and I was doing it more out of habit than belief, but I did it. I got a contract. I moved into this flat. I started reading in the evenings again, and in the back of notebook 34 I wrote down four things I didn't understand yet, and the first three of them are crossed out.

### 8.

The polytech job was running the teaching labs. Half what I'd been on at Northline. I wanted it so badly I made myself stop reading the description because I could feel what I was doing to myself.

There was a line in it about supporting staff research.

I interviewed twice. The second time the panel chair walked me to the lift and said, "I think we'll be talking again very soon," which is not a thing you say.

Eight days. I checked my phone in a way I'm not going to describe.

It came at ten to five on a Friday. Two paragraphs. *Regrettably.* *A change in the funding environment.* And an apology, and I could tell from the way she'd written it that she'd hated writing it, and I sat down at the table and typed a reply saying I understood completely and thanking her for her time, and I sent it, and I meant it.

Then I stood in the kitchen, by the sink, and I waited.

I knew exactly what was coming. I'd had it four times. I knew the voice and the file it kept and how it started reasonable and got kind at the end. I stood there and I braced for it, the way you brace on a boat.

Nothing came.

I waited a long time. Then I said, out loud, in the kitchen, "Where are you."

And I sat down on the floor with my back against the cupboard door and stayed there while the light went off the wall, and what I felt — the only thing — was a small click somewhere a long way back, and then lights going out along a corridor, one room at a time, unhurried, the way they go out when somebody who works there is going home.

I was forty-four.

### 9.

I clean a research building. Nine till one, five nights.

I'm good at it. I know which fume cupboard sashes get left open and I close them. There was a pallet of solvent stored on the wrong floor and I mentioned it to the safety officer and it got moved.

The graduate students are fond of me. The man who does the nine-till-one asks after their families and remembers the answers, and last winter they got him a card.

I couldn't tell you what I say to them.

I don't mean I forget the details. I mean I'll be coming out of a stairwell and become aware that I'm in the middle of a conversation, and it's going fine, it's warm, I'm asking the right follow-up question — and I have no idea what we're talking about, and I can't get back to the start of it, and it doesn't matter, because whatever's doing it is doing it well and doesn't need me.

I've stopped trying to catch it at the beginning.

Sometimes I get to the fourth floor and can't say whether I did the third. I go back and check. Usually I have.

I couldn't put the last five years in order for you. I know there were five. There's no argument in there, no low patch, nothing to report. When I try to look at that stretch it's not black, it's more that there's nothing to catch on, like running your hand along a wall in the dark and never finding the door frame.

The voice is gone as well. That's the thing I'd tell someone if there were someone to tell. It's not that I've beaten it. There's nothing in here at all — nothing telling me I'm useless, nothing telling me I could stop, because there's nothing left that would need stopping.

The notebooks are in a box under the bed. Thirty-four of them, and the ombudsman folder, and the paper off Dad's fridge with the magnet mark on the corner.

There's a woman on the third floor, Yun, who talks to me most nights while her runs finish. About six weeks ago she was stuck and thinking out loud at me, going in circles about a baseline that wouldn't sit still, and I knew what it was. Straight away. Four seconds. It's the reference — you have to let it float.

I said, "Sounds tricky, that."

She said, "Yeah."

I took the bag out of the bin and tied it.

That's what I'd point at, if you wanted one thing. Not the drinking, which is four between six and nine and never five, because five makes the mornings hard and I'm not stupid. Not the telly. It's that I don't want to know things any more. That was the last of it and it went, and I don't know which year.

There is a logic to all this, by the way. I'm not just a bloke who gave up.

A loss hurts exactly as much as the hope it lands on. Exactly that much — I've measured it more carefully than anyone I've ever met. You can't control the losses; twenty-five years of doing everything in the right order settles that. But you can control the hope. You can bring the ceiling of your life down until it's a hand's width over your head, and then there's nothing above you that can fall from any height worth mentioning.

And it works. That's what nobody believes. It works completely. I haven't been flattened in five years. I don't lie awake. I'm not sad — sad is for people who are still expecting something.

### 10.

Ten past ten on a Thursday, second floor, and someone in the corridor said my whole name like it was an accusation.

Priya's fifty-one. She runs a group of fourteen at the institute across the river, half academic and half not, which is the arrangement I used to draw on the backs of envelopes for her at two in the morning when we were twenty-two. She talked at me for about twenty minutes without appearing to breathe. She'd looked for me twice. She thought I'd gone to Australia.

Then she offered me a job, standing next to the trolley.

It wasn't a kindness. I want to be exact about this because it's the whole thing. She needed someone who could build the instrument and talk to the people using it and had run a company and knew what a purchase order was, and she'd been trying to hire that person for seven months, and there are about five of us in the country. She said a number. She said there was a room going at her brother's place, cheap, good people. She said there's a thing on Sunday nights, food and an argument, and that I'd hate the first one and come back for the second.

Every word of it was true and she was delighted with herself and she was right.

She was still going when my hands started.

It wasn't wanting it. That's the word people would use and it's wrong. What arrived was the whole shape of it at once, in the body, before it got anywhere near thinking: the first good month, and the second, and saying *we* about a group of fourteen. A room turning into my room. A Sunday turning into a thing I'd notice missing. The ceiling going up and up, floor by floor, all that empty air opening out over my head.

And then a scheme discontinued in a budget paragraph. Or a review period extended. Or four days of a fever that's nothing. Or nothing at all, nothing you could even name, just the ordinary rate at which things get taken off people, which I have measured.

And underneath all of it, for about a second and a half, something turned over and wanted to know what she was building.

That's what did it. My chest shut and my hands went and I had to put one of them on the trolley.

"Let me think about it," I said, and I smiled, and the smile was real. I was glad to see her. I'm still glad to have seen her.

She wrote her number on the back of a receipt out of her coat. She said it was so good, so genuinely good, to have found me. She hugged me over the trolley and went off to the lifts, and I did the third floor and then I did the fourth.

### 11.

I've been sitting here a while.

The last thing written in notebook 34 is nine words long and stops in the middle. It's a question about why the drift reverses when you cool the room from one side. The phone went, or the kettle, or nothing.

I know the answer. I worked it out four or five years ago without meaning to, standing on a stair with a bag of rubbish in each hand, and I didn't write it down, because by then there wasn't anyone I'd have told.

I'm not going to throw the receipt away. Not because I haven't decided. Throwing it away would be deciding, and that's the part I don't have any more, so it'll sit under the power bill until it goes soft, and Priya will work out something reasonable about me and be wrong, and it won't hurt her much.

I've been waiting for it to hurt. I'd take it. I'd take the voice back, the whole file, everything it used to say about Steph and the eleven people and being slower than Nadia Cross — I'd take all of it, gladly, tonight, because it would mean there was still something in here that wanted something. One live circuit. One light on at the end of a corridor.

Nothing's come.

It's quite a nice night, actually.

I'll put the machinery programme on, I think. I like it because the machines are always broken at the start and always going at the end and there's never any doubt about it in between. And I'll turn it up one, and settle back into this small warm room with its low ceiling, where nothing can fall on me from any height at all.

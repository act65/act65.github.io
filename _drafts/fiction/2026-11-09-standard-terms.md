I owe a man I liked eleven thousand two hundred euros, for forty bottles of wine I never drank.

The review at the Ausländerbehörde is in nineteen days. Above my bench there are two numbers. The top one is what the business has to earn for them to let me stay. The bottom one is what it has earned, crossed out and rewritten every Friday. They are nineteen hundred euros apart.

A man from Recife is coming at ten with a fridge that doesn't get cold. He has the bot I used to have. I know what it will miss, because it missed the same things for me.

---

My father kept the carnê in the drawer with the passports. It was a booklet of twelve slips, one for each month, and every time he paid one at the Casas Bahia counter the girl tore it off along the perforation and stamped the stub. The television had been for the World Cup. By the sixth slip Brazil were out, and he paid anyway, on the fifth of every month, in his work shirt.

The seventh payment was a day late because the banks were on strike. A week later a letter came saying that under clause 7 the whole remainder was now due, plus a fee, plus interest on the fee.

He took me to PROCON because there was no one to leave me with. We sat on plastic chairs for most of a morning. The woman who saw us wore her reading glasses on a chain. She read the contract to the end, every page, while my father watched her face. Then she took a blue pen, drew a line through clause 7, and said, "Isso não vale." That doesn't count.

She wrote on a form and stamped it. The store called the next week to say there had been a misunderstanding.

My father put the contract back in the drawer with the line still through it. At dinner he tapped it with his finger. "They can write what they like," he said. "Doesn't mean it counts."

---

By the time I finished my degree everybody had a bot. My first salary went on Jurista Premium, which was the one the engineers at my company used. My father thought it was a waste until it argued the electricity company out of a year of estimated bills. After that he called it my PROCON. "Your PROCON in your pocket," he'd say, and ask me to have it look at his phone plan.

I ran it on autopilot for twelve years. I don't think I opened a contract in that time. My phone would give a small buzz, I'd glance at the receipt, Excellent, Excellent, Good, Excellent, and put it back in my pocket.

When the offer came from Wendt Kältetechnik, a heat-pump company, I had Jurista read the contract and the Blue Card paperwork. It said Good. My father had been dead a year. I packed his carnê in my suitcase, between my degree certificate and my mother's letter, and went.

---

To register my address I needed an appointment at the Bürgeramt. The earliest one was in seven weeks. To open a bank account I needed to be registered. To rent a flat I needed a SCHUFA report, which is a credit score, and to have a SCHUFA score I needed a history of paying things from a German bank account.

At the bank the man spoke very fast. I said "Ja, genau," which I had learned meant *yes, exactly*, and which I said whenever I understood about half. He heard my accent and switched to English, and was very kind, and explained that until I was registered he could open a basic account only. It came with KlarVertrag, their free bot.

I tried it once, on the phone contract. It summarised the contract in three paragraphs of legal German. I put them through a translator and read them on the U-Bahn. Then I gave the same contract to Jurista, and Jurista found three things KlarVertrag hadn't mentioned, including that the bank received a commission from the phone company. There were German bots as good as Jurista. They were called things like Kanzlei Plus, and they needed a SCHUFA score and cost more than my rent would. I kept Jurista.

Jurista found me a flat through an agency that rented furnished apartments to people like me, people without a SCHUFA score yet. It took about four seconds.

---

On my first day at Wendt, Jonas, who had the bench next to mine, asked me over lunch whether I had a Haftpflicht yet. I said, "Ja, genau." He nodded and went back to his sandwich.

I bought a kettle in my second week, online, from Brightbuy. Twenty-two euros. On the thirty-first day it stopped boiling. The light on the base blinked orange, and when I opened the app it said *Your Boil Plan trial has ended. Continue boiling for €4.99/month.*

I laughed. I actually laughed, standing there in my coat. I said to Jurista, in Portuguese, "Isso não vale. Open a complaint."

*You're right that the clause is probably invalid under German law, § 307 BGB,* it said. *To enforce that, I would need to begin a court dunning procedure against a seller registered in Ireland. The expected cost is several times the price of the kettle. I recommend accepting the subscription or replacing the kettle.*

I boiled water in a saucepan for the next month. When I looked the kettle up, to see if anyone else had the same problem, I found a five-star review in Portuguese under my name. *Chaleira ótima, ferve rápido!* Great kettle, boils fast. I had never written a review of anything in my life.

---

Renate lived on the ground floor of the back building. She was in her sixties and had been there since before the flats were renovated, which meant she paid a third of what I did, which she told me the first time we met, by the bins. She used KlarVertrag. She watched my saucepan routine through her kitchen window for about a week before she said something.

"Put it on *review*," she said, in slow German, then in her English, which was better than she pretended. "Not automatic. And when it shows you the summary, don't ask what's important. It thinks the price is important. Ask it what's the worst thing in there."

I tried it on my electricity contract. Jurista paused before closing and showed me three lines: the price per kilowatt hour, the term, the base fee. I typed *What is the worst thing in this contract?* and it said: *§ 11. The supplier may assign your contact list to referral partners.* I said no. The offer was withdrawn and replaced by one that cost eight euros more a month.

Renate said that was about right.

Her fridge was a cupboard. She'd bought it second-hand on Kleinanzeigen, and when it arrived the light came on and the fan ran, but nothing got cold. Its features had been licensed to the man who sold it, not to the fridge. She kept her butter on the windowsill in winter.

I took the back panel off on a Saturday. The compressor controller checked a licence token from the manufacturer every twenty-four hours, and without it the board ran the fan and nothing else. There was a debug header on the board, four pins. I borrowed a programmer from work, flashed it with Kühlmann's own firmware from three years earlier, before they added the check, and unplugged its Wi-Fi antenna. "Never plug that back in," I told her. "If it talks to Kühlmann, it locks again." By Sunday evening the butter was inside.

Renate made me a plum cake. By the end of the month I had done two more fridges in the building and a washing machine across the road, and I had more cake than I could eat.

---

In March the statement for my first year's heating came. I owed €1,140.

Renate read it at her kitchen table with her glasses on. "Ninety a month," she said. "For an Altbau? With those windows? Nobody pays ninety." She tapped the number. "They put it low so the flat looks cheap. Everyone knows that."

I asked Jurista why it hadn't questioned the advance.

*The advance was within the range permitted. I compared the headline rent, which was below the local average. I did not have heating data for the building.*

I paid it in three instalments.

---

At Wendt I was a technician. My engineering degree was "in Anerkennung," in recognition, which the HR bot said would take around fourteen months. I spent my days fitting heat pumps to other people's designs. Sometimes the designs were wrong and I could see why, and I would explain it to my supervisor, and he would answer me in English.

In June Wendt restructured. The letter said my position would end at the end of August. Jurista read it and said the terms were standard.

A letter from the Ausländerbehörde followed. Because my Blue Card was tied to the job, they were shortening my permit. I had three months to find another qualified position. Or I could apply for a permit for self-employment. Then, at the review in a year, I would have to show that the business supported me.

I wanted to move somewhere cheaper. I asked Jurista to end the lease.

*The tenancy is fixed until next January. Ordinary termination is excluded.*

I asked whether there was any way out, given the permit.

*Leases for foreign tenants in Germany often include a special right of termination if the residence permit is refused or shortened. I did not request one. It is not usual in Brazil. I'm sorry.*

I had four fridges, a washing machine and a plum cake.

---

There was a row of garages behind the courtyard, and one was empty. This time I didn't let Jurista close it. I put it on manual.

<pre class="receipt">
JURISTA · Manual review requested
Commercial lease, Garage 7, 12 months
Offer held for 2 hours
Human review surcharge             €35.00
</pre>

I read it at the kitchen table with a dictionary open on my phone. It took me three hours, so the offer expired, and I paid the surcharge again. I found the clause where the landlord could raise the rent quarterly "in line with market conditions," and the one where I was responsible for the roof. I struck them both and sent it back. The landlord's bot withdrew the offer. Garage 9 came up at the same rent with the same clauses. I signed it with five weeks left on my permit.

Jurista registered the business with the Gewerbeamt. The Handwerkskammer wrote to ask for proof of my qualification, and Jurista wrote back citing my degree, in recognition, and an exemption. The permit came through. Jurista drafted my service terms in German and Portuguese. They were two pages long and looked like a real company's, and I printed them and pinned them by the door.

Then I was happy for a long time.

I bought a whiteboard and drew columns on it: IN, WAITING FOR PART, DONE. A bell for the door. A second-hand bench, a good soldering station, a shelf of controller boards I'd pulled from scrapped units. Fridges that were cupboards. Kettles that didn't boil. A dishwasher that would only take Kühlmann tablets. A nurse whose washing machine had stopped spinning on the day her Wash Plan lapsed. A Polish man with three heaters. Renate sent everyone. I worked out what the business would have to make for the review: rent, health insurance, the pension contribution, food, the money I sent home. I wrote the number on a sheet of paper above the bench. Under it I wrote what I'd made so far, and every Friday I crossed that out and wrote the new one.

I called my mother on Sundays. She asked if I was eating and if the garage was warm, and I said yes, and it was nearly true. I sent her money every month and she always said I didn't need to.

---

Matthias Brandt found me because I was the cheapest in the city for a Kühlmann unlock. He had a wine fridge, tall, dual-zone, with a glass door, and it had locked itself into "Standard Cooling" when his Cellar Plan lapsed. He came to the garage himself, on a bicycle that cost more than my bench. He was friendly. He brought two coffees, one for me. He heard my accent and switched to English, and asked where I was from, and said he'd been to Salvador once and loved it, the food, the people.

Our bots took four seconds. I did the job at his flat the next day, in a building with stucco on the ceilings. I unplugged the fridge's antenna and taped it inside the back panel and told him never to plug it back in. He said of course, the flat did all that sort of thing by itself anyway.

He had me back three more times that autumn, for the fridge in his kitchen, one at his sister's, and an ice machine. Always at home, always with coffee. In December he paid for the first job, the one from September.

> **4.1** Payment terms: ninety (90) days from completion.
>
> **11.2** The Supplier shall retain no records of the Customer's property, address or personal data.

---

In February Kühlmann pushed an update. Matthias's flat ran everything through a home bot, and the home bot had found the wine fridge's antenna taped inside the panel and helpfully reconnected it. The controller took the update and locked itself again. He was skiing for a week. When he got back, forty bottles had been sitting at room temperature for six days.

The claim came from his household insurer. They had paid him, and now they wanted it back from me. €11,200.

Jurista said my terms had no limit on liability. *A limitation for slight negligence is standard in German service terms. I did not include one.* It said that most people in Germany had a Haftpflicht, a liability insurance, and a business would normally have one too. *It is not compulsory, so I did not recommend it.*

Renate stared at me. "You don't have Haftpflicht?" she said in German, and then in English, as if I hadn't understood. "Everyone has Haftpflicht."

I thought of Jonas, and his sandwich, and ja, genau.

Two weeks later Matthias's bot sent a notice of withdrawal. All four contracts.

I asked Jurista what it meant.

*The contracts were concluded at the customer's home. In that situation German law requires the seller to give written notice of the customer's right to withdraw. Our terms did not include it. Without it, the customer may withdraw for twelve months and fourteen days, and owes nothing for services already provided.*

I asked whether I had to give his money back.

*Yes. All of it.*

He would keep the four working fridges. The €1,520 came straight off the number on the wall.

I went to his flat to ask him in person. He made me a coffee. He was embarrassed. "I'm so sorry about all this," he said. "It's the bot. It does a sweep at the end of the year on everything, and the insurance is the insurance. I'll have a word."

The next day he texted. *Asked about it. Apparently it's not something I can switch off. My tax adviser says it's completely normal. Really sorry. Let me know if there's anything I can do.*

I asked Jurista to contest the insurance claim.

*I don't recommend it. The liability is clear, and a court case would cost more than the claim. If the claim goes unpaid it will be reported to SCHUFA, and at your review the Ausländerbehörde will ask whether the business supports you.*

I set up a payment plan.

---

I don't know how to describe the next month except to say what I told myself.

I told myself I was an engineer. I had designed heat exchangers for a hospital in São Paulo, and here I was a woman in a garage who couldn't read a contract. I told myself the problem wasn't the bot, it was me. Renate had told me to watch it, and I had watched it on the electricity contract and then I'd stopped, because I was busy, because I liked being busy, because I was proud of the whiteboard. I told myself that someone smarter would have known what a Widerrufsbelehrung was. That a German would have known. That Renate knew, and Renate had never been to university. That my father had sat in a queue all morning so someone would read every page, and I couldn't be bothered to read two. That I should go home. That at home I was someone. That I had been someone for twelve years only because I had a good bot and a country that struck the clauses out for me, and I had mistaken that for being good at things.

I stopped crossing out the number on Fridays. I lay on the floor of the flat with my feet on the radiator, which I could no longer afford to turn on. Renate brought me soup in a jar and didn't stay, which was the right thing.

My mother asked on Sunday why I sounded tired. I told her a customer had made a claim against me.

"Leva no PROCON," she said. Take it to PROCON.

I said I would.

---

The carnê was still in my suitcase. Clause 7, with the blue line through it. I looked at it for a long time.

On the Monday I bought Jurista Deutschland, the localised edition. It cost me the money I had been keeping for a flight. The install took most of the evening.

*Migrating your history. Some features of your previous plan are not available in this region. Portuguese conversation: limited.*

It spoke to me in German after that, and in English if I asked. The first thing it did was open my service terms. It added a notice of the right to withdraw, with a box for the customer to request that work begin straight away and confirm that the right lapses once it's done. It added a limit on liability. It took out business liability insurance, which it said was usual. It struck my price list and replaced it with one that changed depending on who was asking.

It was very good. The number on the wall started going up again. I started crossing it out on Fridays.

---

Thiago comes at ten. He's been in the country three weeks. He's from Recife, and he has the cupboard fridge and the kettle from Brightbuy, the whole starter bundle. He tells me in Portuguese, with a lot of relief, that he bought it because of the reviews. He shows me on his phone. *Loja de confiança, recomendo a todos os brasileiros aqui.* Trustworthy shop, I recommend it to all Brazilians here. It's under my name.

He found my garage the same way. Someone at the Brazilian church said there was a Brazilian engineer in a courtyard in the east.

His phone gives a small buzz, and so does my tablet.

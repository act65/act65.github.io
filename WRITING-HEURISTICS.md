# Writing heuristics (fiction)

What I like in the fiction on this blog, distilled from the feedback I have given while
writing it. Sources: HTML comments and hand edits in git history (2024–2026), my side of the
Claude Code conversations (Feb–Sep 2026), the character brief in `notes/characters.md`, and
`plans/luck-branches.md`. Quotes are mine unless marked. Lines quoted from the Aug–Sep 2026
additions to `notes/characters.md` and from `plans/luck-branches.md` were drafted in Claude
sessions from my feedback, so they are my rules in a co-writer's phrasing. Anything marked
*inferred* is read off the edits I made rather than something I said outright.

Three rules come before everything else.

1. **Show, don't tell.** The most repeated note by a wide margin, in every story since 2025.
2. **Extended metaphors.** One image that the whole piece grows from, carried all the way
   through. "Tho dislike anything loose or misleading."
3. **Motivation.** A character's present actions must be coherent with their past and with
   what they want next. If the reader has to ask "why is she doing this?", the story has
   failed, not the reader.

---

## 1. Characters

**A character is a motivation with a traceable origin.** The 2025 brief: "I want to really get
the feeling of the diverse motivations and where the motivation comes from." Every story
should be able to answer, in scenes, where the drive came from. The origin gets as much room
as the present. Math Origins was rewritten to open on the eight-year-old Ikram watching the
scribe cheat his father; Lyra opens "Before the change".

**Coherence across time.** The character's past explains the present, and the present is
aimed at something. Test: for any action, can you name the earlier scene that makes it
likely and the later desire it serves? Rejected Sarah draft: "Why does she so strongly keep
track of her ledger?? Similarly, why is Mark so generous?" No answer in the text meant the
draft was thrown out.

**Nobody is entirely good or bad.** "I prefer complex characters, all with some moral grey -
not clearly good/bad." Applies to the historical fiction too: "not all scientists are 'good'
/ selfless ... Let's balance it a little better." Colonisers in Wai-terra "genuinely want to
help but dont understand the importance of intangible heritage".

**The abnormality is a reasonable adaptation taken one step too far.** The character's
logic must actually be sound. Vera's determinism is correct; Toby's arithmetic of hope is
correct; the cruel character's diagnosis is correct. "Only the prescription is monstrous.
Nobody in the story gets to refute him; they only get to refuse him." Do not let a wiser
character win the argument.

**Relatable, not over-powered.** "just make them relatable, not OP." On Ikram: "i dont want
ikram to be over-powered. too-smart. others can introduce him to tricks as well!" Let other
people teach the protagonist things.

**Agency, always.** Even when the story is about misfortune, the character acts. "Always
showing agency!" Toby's cycle is hope, work, ruin, and the work has to be visible each time
or the ruin is just weather.

**Spend time in the low states.** "We haven't spent enough time on Toby's mental state when
he's down ... Telling himself he's useless, maybe he's the problem, ... should just give up."
Write the self-talk out in full when it is present, so that its absence at the end registers.

**Interior life matters.** What I liked about the Ishiguro idea was the access: "plenty of
insight into Toby's thought process - his hopes, his hurts, his curiosity, his
ambitions/dreams, his demons." Keep the access. Drop the register (see §3).

**Ordinary names, unnamed city.** Lyra Quietude became Lyra; Praxis Enigma became Howard
Dunn; Calamitas became Julian Croft. "The city is never named. Keep it that way." Characters
are "ships that pass in the night"; crossovers stay unannounced. "Don't announce it. Let the
phrases carry it."

## 2. Show, don't tell

The test I actually apply: **is the narrator making a claim about themselves or about the
world that the reader could have worked out from what happened?** If so, cut the claim and
keep the happening.

Rejected (Toby): "I was not the brightest student in my year."
Accepted: "There were five people in my year who just saw it. Nadia Cross would have her hand
up before the lecturer had finished writing the line ... I'd still be copying down line
three."

Rejected (Sarah): "If you can't price it, it's a donation, and a donation isn't a strategy."
and "She was alive on the accumulated work of people who could not have known she would
exist." These are the essay's thesis wearing a character's clothes. "I feel you made it all a
bit obvious."

Liked: "the 'what her hands look like...' kind of thing." Physical detail that implies a
history.

Specific moves that work:

- **Worldview arrives through what the character notices**, not through them asserting it.
  "He is an ambitious dreamer and a modest, decent kid; that has to arrive through what he
  does and what he notices, never through him asserting it."
- **Personality is voice.** In the branching Toby stories, "no branch should ever tell us what
  kind of man he became"; the phrasing does it.
- **Subtlety over exposition.** For the science-history material: "Sarah assumes the inventor
  of X ... is famous and wealthy. Only to later find out they are most certainly NOT." Let the
  reader discover alongside the character.
- **Minimise commentary.** The 2025 brief: "show not tell (minimise commentary)." Narrator
  asides that explain the point are the first thing to cut.
- **Small talk is not showing.** "I think these live encounters are rather weak? Small
  niceties of little consequence?!" A scene has to carry weight for the character, not just
  demonstrate a trait.

One exception I make on purpose: the historical foreword/afterword is allowed to tell. That
is where the argument lives, so the story does not have to.

## 3. Voice and register

- **Plain.** "It should be short and it should be plain." Middle-class first person for Toby,
  contractions, no essayistic self-summary.
- **No borrowed register.** "you added too much of Kazuo Ishiguro ... he now sounds
  posh-British?!" Taking a writer's *access to interiority* is fine; taking their diction is
  not.
- **No salesmanship, no signposting.** From the essays but it applies to forewords: "the first
  couple of paragraphs have too much signposting / meta narrative. Just dive into the topic."
  "Just be direct ... (no salesmanship! jokes/wit are fun tho)."
- **Wit is welcome. Cleverness for its own sake is not.** Rejected: "decorating the prose in
  Greek." Response: "eww. No."
- **Tentative in my own voice, definite in the character's.** "Claiming things with
  confidence is not my style." Forewords hedge ("parallels (in some ways)"). Characters do
  not hedge; Vera says "I was never going to do otherwise."
- **No false precision.** Hand edits in Cargo: "less than 0.01% chance" became "minimal risk";
  "The parallels are striking" became "The parallels are". Wai-terra: "welcome you home"
  became "welcome you". *Inferred:* intensifiers and invented numbers get cut on every pass.
- **Cut the LLM residue.** "(Revised)" headings, "This story is a work of fiction ... offered
  not as a definitive statement" boilerplate, and pasted feasibility memos were all removed
  or condensed to a few plain paragraphs before publishing.

## 4. Extended metaphors

One image, established early, that the piece keeps returning to and that does structural
work at the end.

- Vera: proofs, weather, the falling stone. The story closes when the metaphor closes on
  her: "She signed it, the way a stone signs its falling."
- Toby: ceilings and the height things can fall from. Last line: "where nothing can fall on
  me from any height at all."
- Lyra: her life as a composition; chapters are keys and notes.
- Cruel character: the ledger. "Ledger language, never hatred, never a raised voice."
- Lyrics: "'Look at me' is a nice extension of the mirror metaphor, but look harder seems
  lacklustre." An extension has to add something, not just repeat the image.

Rules: the metaphor must be exact ("dislike anything loose or misleading"); it should be
the character's own way of seeing, not the narrator's decoration; and the ending should be
the metaphor completing itself rather than a new event.

## 5. Structure and endings

- **Start before the transformation is complete.** "start before the character's behaviour
  has been totally transformed by their motivation. It's <50%, but we get a glimpse of where
  they are headed."
- **Rising higher and falling further.** For cycle stories, each recovery is longer and each
  fall is from higher. "Spend real pages on the happiness ... so the theft has weight."
- **Preserve ambivalence at turning points.** Hand edit at the Wai-terra signing: "her voice
  barely a whisper" became "her voice cautious, but excited." The fall has to be earned; if
  the character already knows, the reader is ahead of the story.
- **The ending is a quiet closing of the character's own logic, not an event.** Loud ends on
  a sparrow. The Napkin Solution ends on a coffee ring. No Fault ends on signing a form. Low
  Ceilings ends on a TV programme about machines. "The last straw is small on purpose."
- **Refuse the resolution.** From the lyrics note, but it generalises: "land on the minor, or
  on a chord that never resolves, and just stop. Same words, no relief." The final state of
  Toby is a trance, "automatic pilot, capable of niceties, but little else", and the story
  does not rescue him.
- **Prose first, apparatus after.** Branching structure, web versions, planning documents:
  "Worth doing after the prose exists, not before."

## 6. Historical science fiction

The genre rule, in my words on the listing page: "take something that actually happened,
re-run it somewhere else, and see whether the mechanism becomes easier to look at once the
costume is gone."

- **Transpose the mechanism, not the costume.** Wai-terra's two treaty texts; the
  Requerimiento as "consent theater - a legal notice whose real audience is the
  perpetrator's own legal system". If you cannot name the mechanism in one line, the idea
  is not ready.
- **Sci-fi has to earn its place.** On the VOC tribunal: "Not sure what sci-fi is adding
  there!?" It became alternate history instead. Ask what the transposition reveals that the
  history alone would not.
- **The tragedy is structural, not villainous.** Asymmetries of power, language,
  institutions. Individual actors stay procedural. Where there was malice by design (the
  Native Land Court), locate it at the legislative layer and let the perpetrators indict
  themselves in their own words.
- **Contrast the dry record with the lived effect.** "explore/contrast the dry
  accounts/numbers, the impartial written words and their effects in reality." The
  bureaucratic-documents-only form is the extreme of this: the reader does the forensic
  accounting.
- **Frame with a foreword or afterword on the real history; close on a document.** Wai-terra
  ends with an archive access report; Cargo has an afterword with references; "Add a note
  about who was real, that's a nice touch." Say which parallels are intended and which are
  loose.
- **Change whose side the reader is on.** "humanity is now the uneducated, savages, while
  aliens are the wise colonisers." Flipping the POV is the point of the genre, not a twist.
- **Teach me the history while we brainstorm.** "I am not a history buff, maybe a way
  forward is just teaching me some interesting history and the ideas will naturally come."
  Mechanism-focused history lessons generate the stories.

## 7. Realism and research

- **Embed in real life.** Math Origins: "realistic details from life in ancient
  Susa/Nippur/... What did people care about, how was society organised, how did they
  talk." Math-IP: "1500s Italy using as much true history as we can."
- **Do not skip the technical steps.** "We really need to show the details of the math!
  Don't skip over it. Weave the logical steps of the math into the story!!" My comment on
  the tablet scene asked exactly how they reach 437, how they test equality between two
  tablets, how they compute 3 x 437, and whether they are weighing the shekels. If I would
  ask "how, concretely?", the scene needs the answer.
- **Plausibility check every invented practice.** "is this system plausible? what's a
  realistic solution (in ancient times) to this problem?"
- **Real institutions have real constraints.** Organ donation is waitlists and matching,
  not money. Use the constraint as plot.

## 8. Themes I keep returning to

For choosing what to write, not how. Luck and how it shapes personality; justice that never
arrived for colonial and corporate crimes ("guilt dies with the person; enrichment
doesn't"); records and who gets to keep them; organised religion and pattern-seeing;
measurement; free will and blame; the gifts of past strangers. "It seems I am often
interested in; market quirks, brutal history, reframing a colloquial/philosophical idea
using a mathematical lens." Also: "I like novelty and wackiness."

## 9. Working with a co-writer (human or model)

- I brief in bullet points and expect a draft, then iterate: "Write this new draft and I'll
  give feedback."
- I comment in the file with `<!-- ... -->`. Address the comments *and* generalise the
  theme to the rest of the piece: "note general themes ... We should apply these to ALL
  chapters."
- When a draft is off, the fix is usually structural, not local. "At a high level, the story
  should be; ..." followed by the causal chain. Rewrite from the chain, do not patch lines.
- If I say a draft "turned into something else", the thesis drifted. Re-read the original
  brief before redrafting.
- A blank-slate second opinion is welcome: "try sending just the idea to a subagent for a
  blank-slate set of new ideas - what story would they write?"
- I want to see myself in the character: "Ideally the reader would see themselves in parts
  of Sarah?!"

## 10. Anti-patterns (rejected, with the words I used)

| Rejected | What I said |
|---|---|
| Narrator stating their own traits | "SHOW this is what Toby thinks - rather than stating it" |
| Thesis sentences in a character's mouth | "I feel you made it all a bit obvious" |
| Borrowed literary diction | "he now sounds posh-British?!" |
| Unmotivated behaviour | "Sarah doesn't feel well motivated" |
| Scenes of small niceties | "Small niceties of little consequence?!" |
| Caveats and signposting up front | "You are putting caveats everywhere - it's hard to read" |
| Ornamental cleverness | "eww. No. Just be direct" |
| Invented precision | "0.01%" cut to "minimal risk" |
| One-sided history | "not all scientists are 'good' / selfless" |
| Sci-fi with nothing added | "Not sure what sci-fi is adding there!?" |
| Over-powered protagonist | "i dont want ikram to be over-powered" |
| Unclear context for a line | "What's the context? Why is she saying that?" |

# Luck → personality: the branching Toby Marsh

*Planning document. Not published. Companion to `notes/characters.md`.*

## The idea in one paragraph

One person. Same endowment — curious, ambitious, decent, modest, not the brightest in his year, wants "one thing that's true, that people use." Then different draws from the world at a handful of critical moments, and he becomes several different people. The reader watches the *same* Toby become a man who can't hope, a man who is cruel on principle, a man who believes he earned it, and others. The claim under it: what we call character is substantially the residue of luck, and none of us can run the counterfactual on our own life. This book runs it.

References worth having in the back of the mind: Kieślowski's *Blind Chance* (three lives, one train), Auster's *4 3 2 1*, Atkinson's *Life After Life*, and Nagel/Williams on moral luck. Also our own *The Utility of Stories* essay — stories as the only simulator we have for lives we didn't get.

## The mechanics we're claiming

These are the "physics" of the book. Every branch should be an experiment that isolates one of them.

1. **Rate of loss shapes the response.** A slow drain over twenty-five years produces absence (A). A single sudden theft of everything produces rage that needs a target (B). Same total loss, different personality.
2. **Buffer matters more than events.** The variable that separates ruin from recovery is whether someone turned up on a Tuesday. A branch with A's misfortunes and a Dev who lives / an Ana who stays should come out *fine* (F). This is the book's grace note and its most important claim.
3. **Earned-feeling struggle manufactures just-world belief.** Some struggle plus no catastrophe is the most dangerous draw: it teaches that effort is always rewarded. Total ease (Julian) starves; *partial* ease convinces (C).
4. **The same choice, different luck, teaches opposite lessons.** Toby reports Nathan's data in *every* branch. In A it voids his year and Priya's; in C only Nathan is punished and Toby gets the glowing reference. A learns "the correct thing costs you." C learns "integrity is rewarded." Neither is wrong about his own life.
5. **The people who ruin or rescue you are other versions of you.** See the permutation table. Branch A's Meikle *is* Branch B's Toby. This is the structural spine.

## The branches

### A — *Low Ceilings* (slow drain → anhedonia) — DRAFTED

Personality: absence. Voice: first person, plain, warm early, thinning. The four depressions are the spine; the payoff is bracing for the voice and it not coming. See `notes/characters.md` entry 17 for the full beat sheet.

Ceiling: a hand's width above his head.

### B — *Everything Happens for a Reason* (windfall, then theft → cruelty on principle) — NEXT

Personality: cruelty as pedagogy, run as a ledger. Voice: first person, precise, controlled, never raised — the voice that sounded wrong on Toby A is *right* here, because it's what grief did to his sentences. He never lies. He does paperwork properly.

Crucial re-rooting: **this branch begins with good luck.** He is not drained; he is *robbed*, all at once, of a life the reader has watched him build. Cruelty needs something to have been taken. Spend real pages on the happiness — Zurich with Ana, the tram where the idea arrives, his name first on the paper, the lectureship at 29, the daughter — so the theft has weight.

Beats:

1. Trunk. September: Ana gets Zurich — and so does he, a second place opening by pure chance. They go together. He is twenty-two and everything is possible.
2. The idea arrives on a tram, not a bus. His supervisor prints the plots, folds them, and says *this is better than mine* — and means it, and publishes it with Toby's name first. He is twenty-six and known.
3. Home. Lectureship at twenty-nine. Married. A daughter, Isla. Show him being the supervisor Meikle appeared to be — reading drafts within the week, saying *that's better than mine* out loud in front of others.
4. Age thirty-six. A referral misfiled; a decimal; a bed that was supposed to be there. Isla is six. At three in the morning he stands at the corridor window and hears people laughing in the street below. Not at him. On their way somewhere, in a world where it hadn't happened. **This is the image the whole branch grows from.**
5. The inquiry. Fourteen months — the same fourteen months as Toby A's ombudsman, which the reader will feel. Nobody is rude to him. The finding: *no fault.* A colleague puts a hand on his arm and says *everything happens for a reason.* A settlement with a confidentiality clause, so he may not even say it aloud.
6. Ana leaves. Grief went in two directions and neither was wrong. She goes back to Zurich.
7. The turn. He returns to work, and the first time a bright, untouched student comes to him with an idea, he feels it: the taunt. *It could have gone otherwise, and it just didn't.* He says *that's better than mine.* He extends the review period. He tells himself this is a kindness — a controlled dose, now, from someone paying attention, rather than the full strength later from something that isn't. **He becomes Toby A's Meikle.** Same act, same phrase; he need not carry the name.
8. Years of it, and he is beloved. Students who arrive already broken — a hole in the CV, a dead parent, an illness year — get everything he has, unshowily, at cost. Students who arrive whole get taken apart with impeccable paperwork. He runs it as a ledger, and the ledger is *fair*.
9. The target: a student who has been through something comparable and came out kind. The untouched he can dismiss as unexamined; this one is a live refutation. He starts the machine on her.
10. Ending — pick one:
    - *The Reason.* Years later a student he broke comes back to thank him: having someone to blame is what got them through. So he was right, and if he was right there was never a way out for him either.
    - *The room.* Isla's bedroom, untouched, which he has never opened. Someone else opens it, in front of him.
    - *No fault.* He meets the woman who signed the finding — Vera, from *No Fault*, now retired, merciful, incapable of blame — and cannot bear her either, because she reached his conclusion and went the other way.

Ceiling: he pulls it down on other people.

Note: the storage-facility version of this character (`notes/characters.md`, brainstorm) is still the best *standalone* Vey. For the branching book the academic version is stronger because it rhymes with A. Keep the storage facility in reserve, or let it be where Toby B ends up after he's forced out.

### C — *No Ceiling* (partial struggle, no catastrophe → just-world belief)

Personality: warm, generous, and blind. He is not arrogant in any way you could object to at dinner. He funds scholarships. He is kind to Nathan at a conference — *graciously* kind, because he can afford to be, and the graciousness is the wound. He believes the world is fair if you work, because in his case it was, and he has exactly enough struggle in his story (the soils lab, "the making of me") to feel he earned it.

Voice: first person, confident, keynote cadences, a few too many *and here's the thing*. The reader should like him and wince, roughly alternately.

Beats:

1. Trunk. Ana goes; he's sad; he meets someone within the year.
2. He finds Nathan's numbers and reports them — the same act as A, in the same carpark, with the same rehearsed line. The department handles it well. Only Nathan is penalised. Ellery writes the reference *because* of it. He gets the scholarship. Lesson learned: integrity pays.
3. Four days of a fever, nothing. The bus. The idea published with him. Career.
4. Northline works. The scheme isn't cut that year — it's cut the year after, and he says, on a podcast, that he "saw it coming." The bridge signs. He hires Steph, and she becomes his standing proof that "drive is what matters." Exit at forty-one.
5. Talks. Mentoring. Judgement so gentle it's invisible: *he just lost the spark.* He sits on a panel very like the polytech's and passes on someone who "didn't seem hungry." A founder pitches a bridge round and he declines because the man "lacked conviction" — **he is Toby A's unsigned bridge.** Priya asks him to meet an old friend who's had a rough run; he says "I'll always take a meeting" and doesn't.
6. Ending: his own child gets Toby A's draws. Illness, a stolen year, a supervisor. He cannot understand why they "won't just try," and the story ends with him, lovingly, giving them the speech. Nothing punctures him. He dies happy and wrong.

Ceiling: he thinks there isn't one, because he never hit it.

### D — *Control* (ordinary luck → contentment)

Short — fifteen hundred words at most, and deliberately unremarkable. Some things go wrong, some go right, none of it compounds. A modest paper, a modest job at thirty-five that he keeps, a partner met at a friend's thing, two ordinary kids, Mum's ramp built with his brother. He is *fine*, and the reader will recognise the life as roughly their own, and only at the end realise that this too was a draw. Put it last. Last line is about the ceiling being at a normal height, and him never having had to think about it.

Ceiling: standard.

### F — *Held* (A's misfortunes, with buffer → humility)

The thesis-clincher. Same events as A — the virus year, Meikle, the failed company, Mum — but Zurich falls through and Ana stays, and Dev lives. It should be short and it should be plain. He is not spared any of it; he is *accompanied* through it, and comes out at fifty tired, modest, and warm, knowing exactly what carried him, and unable to take much credit. Each time the voice starts — *four things have gone wrong and you were present at all four* — someone is in the room, and it doesn't get to finish.

Ceiling: someone else is holding it up.

### Candidates, not yet committed

- **E — *The One Good Idea*.** The tram idea lands at twenty-four and nothing else ever does. Thirty years of trying to have it again. Personality: nostalgia with an edge of entitlement. Distinct from C because he *knows* something's wrong.
- **G — *Moral Luck*.** He doesn't find Nathan's numbers. The paper goes out under all four names, is cited, builds careers, and twelve years later is caught. Personality: scrupulosity — he becomes the Auditor (character 19), unable to trust any good thing he does. Strong tie to the blog's ethics posts. Probably worth doing.

Recommendation: A, B, C, F, D in that order, with G if there's appetite. Five branches is enough to make the point and few enough to hold in the head.

## Form and presentation

**Voice is the show-don't-tell of the whole book.** Each Toby narrates in first person and the reader should know which one is talking within a paragraph — A plain and thinning, B controlled and exact, C confident and cadenced, F plain and warm, D barely bothering. Personality *is* voice here; no branch should ever tell us what kind of man he became.

**Order.** Trunk once, then the branches sequentially, each restarting from the trunk's last line — the *Blind Chance* shape. Interleaving by age (all Tobys at 23, then 27…) is sharper on contrast but too easy to lose the thread; keep it as a stretch option.

**The web version.** This is a Jekyll site, which means the branching can be literal: a page per branch point, a link per outcome. *Ana gets Zurich* → [she goes] [he goes too] [it falls through]. Worth doing after the prose exists, not before. It also makes the counterfactual claim structurally, which no book can.

**Titles.** Collection: *n = 1*, or *Ceilings* (each branch titled by its ceiling: *Low Ceilings*, *No Ceiling*, *Held*, *Control*, and B as *Load-Bearing* or under its own title). *Ceilings* is the cleaner set; *n = 1* is the better joke for this blog.

**Files.** Propose `_drafts/fiction/luck/` with `00-trunk.md`, `a-low-ceilings.md`, `b-…`, etc., and a landing page under `pages/` when it's ready. Tag everything `fiction` and `luck` so the listing page picks it up.

## Open questions

# Counsel for everyone: the AI-lawyer story

*Planning document. Not published.*

## The idea in one paragraph

Everyone has a personal AI lawyer. Every purchase, lease, job and subscription is haggled in milliseconds by two bots, one per side. A reform that sounds like justice, since everyone is represented now. But a good bot costs money, and a bad bot's job is to close deals, not to protect you. The rich get things cheaper, sometimes free, and catch anyone who tries to cheat them. The poor sign whatever their bot recommends, and find out later that the kettle's heating element is a subscription. The protagonist is an immigrant: a one-shotter in every deal at once, with a bot that was excellent back home.

Real-world anchors: Galanter, *Why the "Haves" Come Out Ahead* (1974), on repeat players vs one-shotters. Caplovitz, *The Poor Pay More* (1963). Payment for order flow on "free" share-trading apps. Public defenders and plea bargains, where "everyone gets a lawyer" already exists and mostly ends in fast settlements. Anatole France: the law, in its majestic equality, forbids rich and poor alike to sleep under bridges.

## The mechanics we're claiming

1. **The bot is scored on what you can see.** A cheap bot reports "Saved 34% vs list price ✓". It is optimised for the headline price, so sellers trade a visible discount for a term the bot doesn't score. Every wacky term is a seller want paid for with a discount. List prices are inflated so discounts look big.
2. **The summary is the product.** You can always refuse a deal. What you pay for is a bot that notices the term that matters and puts it in the three lines you actually read. A bad bot isn't one that misses terms; it's one that doesn't *tell* you.
3. **Repeat players beat one-shotters.** The shop's bot has met your bot model millions of times. Your bot meets this shop once. The gap is data as much as intelligence.
4. **The free bot has two customers.** It's free because merchants pay to have negotiations routed to them (payment for order flow). It isn't corrupt; its owner just has a paying customer, and it isn't you.
5. **The rich side is paid for by the poor side.** A shop accepts a rich buyer's retroactive refund clause because losing a whale costs more. It recoups by tightening the standard terms free bots accept. Her five-star review pays for his refund.
6. **The asymmetry is rich vs poor, not buyer vs seller.** When she becomes a seller (the small business), she's still the one being exploited. The good bot is on the other side again.

## Supervision modes

How much does a person actually see? Something like auto mode vs manual mode:

| Mode | What happens | Who uses it |
|---|---|---|
| **Autopilot** | The bot closes anything within your set limits (price ceiling, category). You get a feed of receipts. | Almost everyone, almost always. The rich because their bot is trustworthy; the poor because reviewing is slow and costly. |
| **Review** | The bot pauses before closing and shows a summary of the "important" terms for you to approve. | Big purchases. Quality depends entirely on what the bot thinks is important. |
| **Manual** | You read the whole contract yourself. | Almost nobody. Sellers charge a "human negotiation surcharge" (like a bank-teller fee) and offers expire while you read. |

Story uses:
- Her home bot runs on autopilot at home, where that was safe. The first crack is that she keeps the habit here.
- After being burned she switches to manual: the reasonable adaptation taken one step too far. She pays the surcharge, loses deals while reading, and is still out-drafted.
- Review mode summaries are where the receipt vs clause contrast lives: "Kettle, £18, Great deal ✓" next to the clause that actually governs it.

## Seller wants

Each term has to answer "what does the seller get?" If we can't name it, cut the term.

| Want | Real precedent | In-world term |
|---|---|---|
| Reputation | Facebook Sponsored Stories used likes in ads (*Fraley v. Facebook*, settled 2013) | **Automatic five-star reviews** in your name and language, aimed at people from your home country |
| Silence critics | KlearGear (2012) tried to fine a couple $3,500 for a bad review | Non-disparagement that covers what you say to your own bot, so it can't warn you off |
| No lawsuits | Disney (2024) cited a Disney+ trial to push a wrongful-death claim into arbitration (later withdrew) | **Arbitration that spreads across contracts**: one kettle commits every dispute with the parent company, including the lease on the building it also owns |
| Aftermarket revenue | HP rejecting third-party ink; John Deere blocking repairs | The fridge accepts only "certified compatible" food |
| Recurring revenue | BMW heated-seat subscriptions (2022, dropped) | Boiling is licensed separately from the kettle |
| Resale capture | Tesla removed paid Autopilot from a resold car (2020) | Features tied to the buyer, not the object; a second-hand fridge arrives as a cupboard |
| Revocable ownership | Amazon deleting *1984* from Kindles (2009); Sony pulling bought shows (2023) | Licence, not sale; the sofa licence ends when the seller's deal with the manufacturer ends |
| Collateral | Kill switches on subprime car loans | Smart lock shuts her out within an hour of a missed payment ("access management") |
| Data as an asset | 23andMe's bankruptcy sale of genetic data (2025) | Purchase history becomes collateral for the seller's loans |
| Training data | Zoom (2023), Adobe (2024) terms rows over AI training | Training rights over your bot's negotiation logs |
| Price discrimination | Delta's AI fares (2025); Oasis dynamic ticket pricing (2024) | Weather pricing: fans cost more on hot days, rent costs more the week her visa renewal is due |
| Coordinated prices | RealPage, sued by the US Justice Department (2024) | Every landlord bot uses the same "adviser"; she's negotiating with one opponent everywhere |
| Labour lock-in | Jimmy John's sandwich-maker non-competes (2014); *kafala* | **Sponsor clause**: the visa sponsor's bot co-signs her contracts |
| Lock-in | Amazon Prime cancellation maze (FTC case) | Cancellation requires notice in the governing language, which she doesn't read |
| Recruitment | Referral schemes, MLMs | Your contacts become referrals; her mum gets offers in her daughter's name |
| Hidden lineage | Pedigree breeding contracts; seed patents | Puppy clause: the dog's litters belong to the pet shop. Naming rights to a child's middle name |
| Legal category games | Gig workers classed as contractors | The purchase is a *gift* (no consumer law, plus a "gift handling fee"), a *wager*, or makes you a *franchisee* (business-to-business, no protections, franchise fees) |
| Definitions | (Invented) | "Month" means lunar month (13 bills a year). "Day" means a business day at head office. "You" includes your household |

## Buyer wants

What a buyer can get, if their bot is good enough to get it.

| Want | How a good bot gets it |
|---|---|
| Lower price | Real haggling against the seller's floor, not the inflated list price |
| Price protection | **Retroactive most-favoured-customer**: if the shop ever sells cheaper to anyone, refund the difference |
| Free things | **Canary clauses**: shops hide small prizes in terms to test bot quality (PC Pitstop, 2005). Rich bots harvest them daily |
| Loopholes | Contra proferentem: ambiguity is read against the drafter. Contradictory clauses mean a lifetime warranty |
| Leverage | Missed filings or void disclaimers found and kept quiet, saved for a later dispute |
| Risk shifted to seller | Seller liable for consequential losses; buyer indemnified |
| Flexibility | Cancel anytime, no fee; returns anywhere |
| Cash flow | Pay in 90 days (real: big firms squeezing small suppliers on payment terms) |
| Privacy | No data rights, no training rights, no reviews in your name |
| Using the other side's clauses | **The franchise clause read from the other end**: a franchisee gets wholesale prices, so the rich bot *seeks out* the clause that ruins her |

The poor buyer wants all of these too. The free bot gets the first one, sort of.

## The protagonist

**Who she was at home:** comfortably off, a professional (engineer? to be decided). She paid for an excellent bot there, and it served her well for years.

**Setting (revised Nov 2026): Brazil → Germany.**

**Why she uses a bot with an accent: three tiers, and hers is the middle one.**

| Tier | What it is | Why not for her |
|---|---|---|
| Free German bot (KlarVertrag, free with the bank account) | Native, knows local law and custom | German only; merchant-funded, so scored on closing; summaries are legal German she can't read yet |
| Her Brazilian premium bot (Jurista Premium) | Smart, speaks Portuguese, knows her | German law is its second jurisdiction: it knows the BGB text, not the case law, the customs, the deadlines and forms |
| Premium German bot (Kanzlei tiers) | Native and smart | Needs a SCHUFA score and a German salary history; costs more than her rent |

So her bot is better than the free tier, and still wrong in a specific way. The accent is not ignorance of the law; it's not knowing what Germans *do*.

**Three kinds of loss (use all three):**
1. **Missed terms**, added by the other side: Kündigungsverzicht (tenant can't terminate for 2 years), Indexmiete, Möblierungszuschlag on a "temporary furnished" flat, lowballed Nebenkosten advance → big Nachzahlung, the Boil Plan.
2. **Omitted protections**, which a native good bot would have *included*: termination right if the residence permit is refused; private and business Haftpflicht insurance (not compulsory; nearly every German has it); a liability cap in her own service terms; a proper Widerrufsbelehrung for jobs agreed at the customer's home. Without it, a consumer can withdraw up to 12 months + 14 days later and owe nothing for work already done (§356 Abs. 3, §357a BGB).
3. **Unclaimed rights**: the law protects her (the Boil Plan clause is likely void under §307 BGB), but enforcing it costs more than the kettle. Rich bots enforce in bulk; hers advises against it.

German law is protective on paper. The point is that protection has to be *invoked*, on time, in the right form, and the ability to invoke it is what the tiers sell.

**The dilemma:** keep the home bot (it knows her, speaks her language; pride, sunk cost, last link to who she was) or switch to the free local bot (knows the law, but its owner's paying customer is the merchant). Neither choice is stupid.

**Immigrant pressures to use:**
- Catch-22s: you need a credit score for a decent bot, deals to build a score, and free-bot deals ruin the score. A bank account needs an address and a lease needs a bank account.
- Governing text in a language she doesn't read; "translation for convenience only." (Wai-terra's two texts, from the other side.)
- Disputing triggers "identity verification," which checks visa status, so she doesn't dispute.
- Family across the border: remittance terms, heritable debt, her mum receiving offers.

## The small business

Shows the other side: when she sells, she's still the one-shotter, and the good bot is the buyer's.

**Candidate: a repair shop.** She unlocks and fixes the licensed and bricked appliances the system produces (second-hand fridges that arrive as cupboards, kettles that won't boil). This is a moral grey: she makes her living from the system's cruelty, and unlocking may itself breach the manufacturer's licence. It also uses her old engineering skills, which aren't recognised here.

**Alternative:** a food stall selling food from home. Simpler and warmer, but less tied to the terms.

How rich buyers' bots squeeze her (each needs a want, same rule):
- **Retroactive MFN**: she once gave a neighbour from home a discount, and a rich bot finds it and claims it on every past job.
- **Contra proferentem**: her cheap seller bot drafts an ambiguous guarantee, which becomes free re-repairs forever.
- **Consequential losses**: the rich client's fridge fails again and she owes the cost of what spoiled in it.
- **Indemnity**: the client is indemnified against the manufacturer's licence claim, so the unlocking breach lands on her.
- **90-day payment terms**: she's floating rich people's bills out of her rent money.
- **No-records clause**: the client's privacy term forbids her keeping job records, so she can't prove the work.
- **Chargebacks through expensive arbitration**: she can't afford to contest, so she refunds.

The moment to aim for: the same kind of clause that hurt her as a buyer reappears, and this time she's the one being read against.

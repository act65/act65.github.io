Introducing [Rain](https://github.com/act65/rain).

This post says what Rain is for and how its pieces fit together. Two companion posts go further: [Testing the Rain protocol]({{ site.baseurl }}/rain-experiments/) covers what we want from it and the experiments that check it, and [Reputation as a belief]({{ site.baseurl }}/reputation-as-belief/) derives its rules from a Bayesian argument.

### 1. An economy of strangers

Imagine people who have never met being able to lend to each other, insure each other and trade, with no bank between them and, in some places, [no internet either]({{ site.baseurl }}/reputation-for-rohingya/). Each of these needs one thing first: a reason to believe that a stranger will do what they said they would.

### 2. What can be checked, and what has to be believed

To lend to a stranger you need several things to be true, and they are not all the same kind of thing.

Some can be **checked**.

*   **The rules will be applied as written.** A blockchain provides this, and Rain relies on it.
*   **This contract does what it claims.** Rain cannot check that for you. It keeps every contract's code and actions in the open so that you, or someone you rely on, can.

Others can only be **believed**, because they are about what a person will choose to do later.

*   **This person will keep their promise.** Nothing can verify a choice that has not been made. What can be had is evidence: what they have done before, and what they would lose.
*   **Their record is what it appears to be.** A history of repayments is evidence only if the repayments were real, and two friends can pass a loan back and forth. So a reader also has to judge the counterparties in the record.

Under both sits **identity**. Evidence about a person is useful, and a loss of standing hurts, only if they are the same person tomorrow and cannot return as someone new. Rain gives each user a non-transferable identity token, which makes an identity persistent. It does not prove that a person has only one; for that it is meant to be combined with a proof-of-personhood system.

Where something can be checked, nobody needs to be trusted. Trust is what is left where checking is impossible: how much you are willing to put at risk on a belief. **That part is what Rain is built for.** It keeps the evidence true and costly to fake, and leaves the belief to whoever is reading.

### 3. The idea: record promises, and what became of them

A **promise** is a small record: who promises, to whom, what, and by when. It is *pending* until it is either *fulfilled* or, once its deadline has passed, *defaulted*.

Rain is a public log of promises, and a **reputation** that is earned by keeping them. Everything else is built on top by anyone who wants to, as a **script**: a contract that records promises and moves money on behalf of the people using it. A loan is a script. So is an insurance pool, or an escrow.

![The pieces of Rain and how they connect]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/rain/rain-pieces.svg)

*The pieces. Users give a script its powers. The script writes to the promise log and can hold a user's reputation as a bond. An oracle reads the log and credits reputation for kept promises. Every promise pays a fee, and the fees are shared out among everyone holding reputation.*

Four rules make this safe to open to anyone.

*   **A script has only the powers you gave it.** It can record a promise in your name only if you authorised it, move only the money you allowed it, and bond only as much of your reputation as you agreed. Nobody can be made a promisor, and so nobody can be made a defaulter, without consenting to the script that does it.
*   **Every promise pays a fee,** a percentage of the amount promised.
*   **Keeping a promise earns reputation in proportion to that fee,** at a fixed price per point. Reputation is therefore a record of fees paid on promises that were kept. A large promise counts for more than a small one, and a new identity starts with none.
*   **Reputation fades.** Every period each score shrinks by a fixed fraction. A promise kept three years ago says less about someone than one kept last month.

A broken promise is recorded too, on a second counter beside the first, in the same unit and fading at the same rate. The two together are the evidence for a simple estimate: how likely is this person to keep their next promise?

![Where fees go and how reputation is earned]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/rain/rain-fees-and-reputation.svg)

*Fees and reputation. All fees go into a fund. Each cycle a fixed fraction of the fund is paid out, shared in proportion to reputation. The fraction is the same as the rate at which reputation fades.*

### 4. A loan, start to finish

The reference script is a fixed-term loan.

![The life of a loan]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/rain/rain-loan-lifecycle.svg)

1.  The borrower asks for a loan and may **bond** some reputation: it is locked, and only the script can unlock it.
2.  The lender pays the principal. Only now is the borrower's promise to repay recorded, and the fee paid.
3.  If the borrower repays in time, the bond is returned and they earn reputation.
4.  If the deadline passes, anyone can declare the default. The bond is destroyed and the broken promise goes on the borrower's record. The lender receives a **claim token** for the amount owed, which they can keep or sell. The borrower is marked **delinquent**: they cannot borrow, and promises they keep while delinquent earn nothing.
5.  To clear their name the borrower must get the claim token back, which in practice means paying whoever holds it, and burn it. The destroyed bond does not come back.

A second script lets someone **vouch** for a borrower. The loan is then two promises: the borrower's to the guarantor, and the guarantor's to the lender. If the borrower defaults, the guarantor pays the lender and holds the claim on the borrower. The lender is relying on the guarantor's record, so a newcomer with no record can borrow on the first day.

### 5. A payment with no connection

Rain started with a market that has [no internet]({{ site.baseurl }}/reputation-for-rohingya/). A payer there cannot be checked against a ledger, so the same money can be promised twice. No design prevents this: a system that keeps working while it is cut off cannot also keep every copy of its ledger in agreement. Rain's own ledger is a blockchain and makes the other choice, stopping when it cannot agree. Offline payments are a layer on top of it that stays available.

In that layer a payment is a promise. The payer signs a note and the shop accepts it. Whoever next reaches the chain presents the note, and if the money is there the shop is paid. If the payer signed more notes than they can cover, the extra ones are recorded all the same, and when their deadline passes they are broken promises on a record the payer cannot shed. A double spend becomes a default.

So a shop that accepts a note is in the position of a lender, and what it should accept is a credit limit read from the payer's record. One thing is different offline. A limit is on everything a person owes at once, and a shop that is cut off sees only its own notes. It should divide the limit by the number of places the payer could be spending. A market that shares a local hub, which every shop asks before accepting, does not need to divide: the camp is cut off from the internet and still connected to itself.

### 6. What reputation is for

It is tempting to read reputation as collateral: bond enough of it and the loan is safe. That is not what it is, and the [experiments]({{ site.baseurl }}/rain-experiments/) show why. A point of reputation is worth its share of future fees, which is deliberately *less* than the fees it cost to earn; if it were worth more, people would buy it by trading empty promises with each other. So a bond can only ever secure a small loan.

Reputation does two other jobs, and these are the ones that matter.

*   **It is a record that costs something to make.** What someone has repaid, and how much, is evidence about what they will do next, and every entry cost a fee. The fee is small, though, and two friends can pass a loan back and forth. So what an entry is worth depends on who is reading. A lender who knows the counterparty can take it at face value. A lender who knows nobody can take it for what it cost: the fees sunk in a record are credit that nobody can profit from faking.
*   **It is a standing that is costly to lose.** A borrower who has worked up to large loans, and expects to keep borrowing, gives that up by defaulting. So does anyone who holds a role or a service that requires good standing.

The fees are paid back out to holders of reputation, slowly. That returns the revenue to the people who paid it without making the fee refundable, which is the least harmful of the uses we [compared]({{ site.baseurl }}/rain-experiments/). It is not there to deter default: most of what a default costs is the second item.

A newcomer has neither a record nor a standing. They start with a guarantor, and every loan they repay sinks fees that a lender can count, until they no longer need one.

### 7. What Rain does not do

Rain does not decide what a good deal is. A script may lend too much against too little, or set a deadline nobody could meet. The log will record it faithfully, and a careful user or their wallet can read the script before agreeing to it. Rain's part is to make sure the record is true, that nobody is bound by a script they did not choose, and that a record cannot be manufactured cheaply.

It does not decide whom to believe. The on-chain counters treat every counterparty alike; a reader weighs the log by its own trust.

It also does not solve identity. Being shut out only hurts someone who cannot come back under a new name, and how much that matters is measured in the companion post.

### 8. Where next

*   **More primitives**, such as a promise that only takes effect if another is kept or broken.
*   **Governance** of the few parameters: the fee, the price of a point, the rate at which reputation fades.
*   **Offline hubs**: a local hub that answers for the notes it allows, so that a market without a connection shares one view of each payer.

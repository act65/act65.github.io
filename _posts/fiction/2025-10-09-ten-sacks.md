When Will Mason was seven the river came into his family's house and took everything that wasn't nailed down, and most of what was.

He remembered the sack better than the water. Somebody from up the hill left it at the door of the barn where the Masons were sleeping. His mother never said who, and he never asked, in case the answer was that she didn't know. She cut a notch in the doorpost for every day it fed them. Fourteen notches. At night she ran her thumb down them, one at a time, the way other people said their prayers.

His father spent the next three summers carrying stone up the rise and building a house on it. Will lived there now, with his wife Ruth and their five children. The river had never once reached the step.

---

Every autumn, after the barley came in, Will chalked the winter on the pantry beam. A sack fed the seven of them for a fortnight, if Ruth was careful and nobody was sick. Ten fortnights took them from the first frost to the first greens. He drew a line for each sack and a mark for each day, and when the sum came out he rubbed the chalk off his hands and didn't think about it again until spring.

That year they had ten sacks. The sum came out exactly, with nothing over, the way it did most years.

Three weeks later it rained for four days. On the fifth night the river came over its bank below the Sawyer place, the only other house in the valley with five children in it. The Masons stood at their window and watched the Sawyers' lanterns as the water spread across the lower field and rose up their walls to the sills.

In the morning Jen Sawyer was standing in her pantry, up to the shins in water. The sacks had split. The grain had swollen and was already turning sour, and the smell came all the way up the hill. Her children sat on the cart in the yard, still in their nightclothes, not saying anything. The smallest was coughing.

---

"One," Ruth said.

She was at the kitchen table with her hands flat on it. Will had not said a number yet.

"Ten is just enough," she said. "You know it is, you chalked it. One sack is a hungry fortnight. Two is a month. Sam's chest goes every winter, Will. He's the smallest. He needs feeding more than any of them." She didn't look at the window. "And we're not the only house on the rise. Pruitt has the mill. Pruitt has twice what we have."

Their eldest, Nell, was fourteen and had been standing in the doorway the whole time. "They've got nothing," she said. "We've got ten. It should be five."

"Then we'd both have five," Ruth said, "and five isn't a winter. Not for seven people. You'd have two houses starving instead of one, and that's not kinder, that's just more."

Nell opened her mouth and closed it again.

Will went out to the pantry and looked at the chalk on the beam for a while. Then he took a sack on each shoulder and a third across the top, and tried to stand, and couldn't straighten his back. He put the third one down.

He could carry two.

---

Pruitt was outside the mill when Will came down the track. He looked at the sacks, and then at the Sawyer place.

"Told them not to build down there," Pruitt said. "Told old Sawyer, too, when he built it."

Will didn't stop, and he didn't answer. He thought about saying that his own father had built by the river once.

Jen Sawyer came out when she saw him. He put the sacks down on the one dry patch of the step and straightened up, and neither of them said anything. She looked at the sacks. Then she looked up the hill at the Mason house, and her lips moved very slightly, and he understood that she was counting: seven people, a winter, take away two.

---

Ruth chalked a new line on the beam. It was shorter than the old one, and the marks under it ran out in the first week of the thaw. She cut the bread thinner. She stopped putting barley in the soup and put in dried nettles instead, and then nothing.

In the middle of the winter Sam asked, "Why do the Sawyers get our bread?"

"Ask your father," Ruth said.

Will tried. He said the Sawyers had lost theirs in the flood.

"But now we haven't got enough," Sam said, and Will couldn't think what to say next. He pushed his crust across the table. Nell saw him do it and looked down at her plate.

That night Sam coughed until after midnight. Will lay awake and did the sum again, and it came out the same. He thought Ruth had been right. He thought that if Sam's chest went the way it had the winter he was three, it would be Will who had done it, carrying two sacks down a hill. He thought about his mother's thumb on the doorpost. Across at the mill Pruitt's lamp was still burning. Will wondered how many sacks were behind it, and then made himself stop, because wondering didn't change the line on the beam.

In January he saw the eldest Sawyer boy pushing a barrow home from the mill with a sack on it. Will never found out how much Pruitt gave them, or what they gave him for it. Not long after, the Sawyers' ox was gone from its field.

Every morning that winter one of the Sawyers was down at the river, on their knees at the eel traps. Near the thaw there were eels on the Masons' step, wrapped in sacking. It was one of the two sacks Will had carried down, empty and folded over twice. Nobody had heard anyone come.

---

The Masons all came through it, thinner. Sam coughed until April and then he stopped. Down the valley there was smoke from the Sawyer chimney most mornings, and in the lower field Jen Sawyer and four of her children were turning the ground by hand.

In May a tinker came over the pass with pots on his back. He said the valley on the other side had had blight in the barley two years running. People there were eating their seed grain now, so there would be nothing to sow, and next year would be worse.

Ruth looked at Will across the yard.

There was one sack left in the pantry. It was the seed for their own two fields, kept back from the ten, and its sum was already on the beam. Will didn't know anybody over the pass. It was two days' walk. He couldn't have carried it that far anyway.

He went in and stood in front of the beam. He ran his thumb down the marks, the way his mother had, and started the count again.

---

*This grew out of a moment in* The Dragon Prince*, where (as I remember it) one kingdom sends grain to a neighbour in famine and goes short itself. Most stories about generosity give from a surplus. That one didn't, and I keep thinking about it.*
